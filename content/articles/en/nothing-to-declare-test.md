Papers Please marked a whole generation of players with its border-inspector formula, but also with its deliberately gray, retro aesthetic, that austere bygone era that suited its oppressive subject. Nothing To Declare starts from the same idea, control and inspection, but takes the complete opposite tack on form: here, no pixelated nostalgia or Soviet misery, but a soft, colorful, cute world. The bet is enticing, and it works on the charm front. There remains the question that decides everything: under this gentleness, is the depth that made Papers Please great there? The answer is more mixed than the game's smile lets on.

![Nothing To Declare, border inspection in a soft, colorful version](/images/nothingtodeclare/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Nothing To Declare is a first-person inspection simulation game, available on PC. You play a security agent who weighs and scans luggage, asks travelers questions, and searches bags when in doubt to hunt down dangerous items. All in a warm, good-natured setting, claimed as cozy, cute and family-friendly, light-years from the grayness of its illustrious model. You earn money, upgrade your workstation, and even cross paths with a sniffer dog named Cookie to flush out illicit substances. The game unfolds several locations, from the port to the airport by way of the parcel office, to vary the settings and situations.

## The gameplay: charm, and a hint of dilemma

The first thing that strikes you is the form. Nothing To Declare fully owns its cozy stance, with modern, clean and endearing graphics that never try to ape a retro aesthetic. It's a refreshing choice, making the game immediately welcoming and relaxed, where Papers Please cultivated anxiety. The core loop, weigh, scan, question, search, is legible and satisfying, and the addition of Cookie the dog brings a touch of gentleness doubled with a concrete mechanic to spot contraband. Progression through money and workstation upgrades gives a clear throughline, and moving from one location to the next pleasantly renews the setting.

Above all, the game doesn't entirely forget the moral dimension that was its model's spice. Some travelers try to bribe you for special treatment, and it's up to you to decide: accept the bribe, refuse it, or stay loyal to the rules. This is where Nothing To Declare recovers an ounce of the tension that gave Papers Please its depth, that dilemma between self-interest and duty. The mechanic exists, it works, and it proves the game isn't content to be a cute, empty simulation. At its best moments, it manages to marry its gentleness with a genuine little touch of conscience.

![Nothing To Declare, the sniffer dog Cookie and the bribe dilemmas](/images/nothingtodeclare/screenshot-2.webp)

## What sticks

Yet this is where the game shows its limits. All of Papers Please's strength lay in its oppressive atmosphere, the pressure of the numbers, the guilt of turning away a family over a misplaced comma, the survival of your own loved ones hanging on every decision. By choosing cozy and good-natured, Nothing To Declare mechanically sacrifices that moral tension and gravity that gave the genre its density. The bribe dilemmas are pleasant but stay light, with no consequences that truly weigh, and the whole feels more like a pleasant, relaxed routine than a real weight on your shoulders. The depth is there in dotted lines, not in depth precisely, and whoever comes for the intensity of Papers Please will be left wanting.

The other reservation is replayability and variety. This type of game lives on the diversity of its situations, the twisted cases, the memorable travelers and the dilemmas that pile up. While Nothing To Declare varies its settings with its three locations, its scanning-and-searching loop stays fairly repetitive, and the interest dulls once the initial charm fades and the workings are well in hand. It's a game to savor in short sessions rather than devour in one go, whose value lies mostly in its atmosphere and accessibility more than in a mechanical or narrative richness that would last over time. A successful cozy game, but a cozy game above all.

![Nothing To Declare, the inspection loop across several locations from port to airport](/images/nothingtodeclare/screenshot-3.webp)

## What we take away

Nothing To Declare is a charming success that dusts off a genre marked by austerity. Its cozy stance and its modern, cute graphics make it a welcoming gateway to inspection simulation, where Papers Please could put people off with its darkness. The core loop is legible and satisfying, Cookie the dog is an endearing find, and the presence of bribe dilemmas proves the game doesn't entirely ignore the moral dimension that makes the genre rich. For whoever seeks a relaxed, benevolent and accessible experience, it's a genuinely good time, gentle and sincere.

You just have to know what you're coming for. To answer the opening question frankly: no, the depth isn't quite there, not on the level of Papers Please anyway. By choosing gentleness, Nothing To Declare put away the moral tension, the pressure and the consequences that gave the genre its density, and it owns a pleasant routine more than a real test of conscience. It's an excellent cozy inspection game, cute and endearing, but it's a cozy game, to approach for its charm and relaxation rather than for the depth of its model. Taken for what it is, it keeps its promise with a smile.

## Verdict

An adorable Papers Please with modern graphics, charming and accessible, but one that trades its model's moral tension and depth for a pleasant cozy routine.

**Strengths:**

- A cozy stance and refreshing modern graphics
- Cookie the sniffer dog, an endearing find and concrete mechanic
- Bribe dilemmas that keep an ounce of morality
- A welcoming, accessible gateway into the genre

**Weaknesses:**

- Gentleness at the price of the tension and consequences that made Papers Please
- A fairly repetitive scanning-and-searching loop over time
- Mechanical and narrative depth below its model
