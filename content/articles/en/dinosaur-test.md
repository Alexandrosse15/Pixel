Your instinct is right, and it is confirmed the moment you read the store page: Dinosaur looks a great deal like a tech demo. Handsome creatures, an island that renders nicely, and behind it very little to do.

Which leaves your real question, the only one that matters: what does it add to the genre since the Zoo Tycoon of our childhood? The honest answer is probably nothing, but not for the reasons you would think.

![Dinosaur, the dinosaur island sandbox](/images/dinosaur/screenshot-1.webp)

## What the game actually offers

The programme announced by studio Dinosaur Master fits in a few lines, and that is already a signal. You create your island with an editor presented as having no limits or restrictions. You populate it with more than fifty dinosaur species, herbivores, predators or pterosaurs. Each species has specific needs, and you have to ensure mutual symbiosis and prosperity. Finally, you can take direct control of any dinosaur.

That is all. And that brevity is not a communication oversight, it fairly describes the project's ambition.

## Why this is not a Zoo Tycoon

The gap needs measuring, because it is structural rather than cosmetic.

What held Zoo Tycoon and its prehistoric expansions together was not the animals, it was the economy around them. Visitors to satisfy, tickets to sell, staff to hire, enclosures to secure, a budget constraining every decision, and that constant tension between animal welfare and park profitability. The dinosaur was only the pretext for a management problem.

Here, none of that is announced. No visitors, no finances, no staff, no security. You build an island, you put creatures in it, you watch over their balance. That is an ecological sandbox, not a management game, and the two do not deliver the same pleasure. The first is contemplated, the second is solved.

The genre found its benchmarks long ago, with Jurassic World Evolution handling precisely the park side, and projects like Prehistoric Kingdom on the detailed animal park front. Against them, Dinosaur is not in the same category, and it does not claim to be.

![Dinosaur, the species and symbiosis on the island](/images/dinosaur/screenshot-2.webp)

## What might still be worth a look

Two things, and it would be unfair to ignore them.

The first is the unrestricted editor. Sandbox players know total freedom of terraforming has value in itself, and many park games frustrate precisely through their grids and constraints. If the tool is genuinely flexible, it will find its audience.

The second is direct control of creatures. Switching from the manager's viewpoint to the tyrannosaur's is something the genre's leaders do not offer, and it is the only genuinely distinctive argument on the page. That said, we have seen many games announce this kind of feature without giving it a reason to exist beyond the curiosity of the first ten minutes.

## What we do not know

The game is out today with no user reviews, so the score above is our assessment rather than a player consensus. It penalises the absence of any management layer and a proposition that fits in a few lines, while crediting the free island editor and direct creature control, two real but insufficient arguments. It is offered in English only.

The real unknown is the depth of the ecological simulation. A game betting everything on symbiosis between species must model needs, food chains and the consequences of imbalance seriously, otherwise all that remains is a decorative zoo where you place handsome models. On that point the store page says nothing, and that is exactly what we would need to know.
