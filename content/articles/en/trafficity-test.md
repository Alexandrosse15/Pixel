Anyone who has played Cities: Skylines knows the moment: you spend two hours building a city, then twenty untangling an interchange. Trafficity starts from an honest observation. For many players, that is the fun part. So why not keep only that?

The city economy is stripped to the bare minimum: residents who want to get to work and to shops, factories that need raw materials and workers, neighbourhoods that hate noise and pollution. Each level gives you a batch of buildings to place. Your job is to get everyone moving.

![A looping highway interchange by a lake](/images/trafficity/screenshot-3.webp)

The simulation is serious. Every car is an agent that picks the fastest route while accounting for congestion, shifts lanes to avoid queues, and gets into the right lane ahead of time. You can tune traffic light phases, create protected left turns, edit lane connections. And road hierarchy emerges by itself: you end up building slip roads and interchanges because the simulation makes them necessary, not because the game asks for them.

![Setting a traffic light's phases, lane by lane](/images/trafficity/screenshot-5.webp)

The problem is the tooling. No undo button. You cannot remove a single lane from a road without demolishing all of it, move a building, or simply upgrade a road from two to four lanes. Right-angle turns are fiddly, roundabouts only come in three sizes. And in large cities, the frame rate sometimes drops sharply when a road snaps. In a game entirely about laying roads, that is a central flaw, not a detail.

Hence the 55% positive reviews on launch day, with many players writing that it feels more like Early Access than a full release.

![A grid city spreading out around a roundabout](/images/trafficity/screenshot-1.webp)

There is something addictive here all the same, and the game is lovely in its minimalism, with a relaxed soundtrack recorded in a studio by Max and Ruby Jones. If Onramp Games adds the missing tools, it will be an excellent game. At 13.04 euros today, it is a gamble.
