Legion Breaker costs 5.75 euros, and its store page promises one thing: begin with a single spell and end with a holy army fighting beside you.

There is an honesty in that simplicity I find restful, in an autumn where everybody is selling narrative engines and cross-cutting skill trees.

![Legion Breaker, the wizard and his army](/images/legionbreaker/screenshot-1.webp)

## The loop

You start with one spell. You kill skeletons. You earn gold.

With that gold you unlock new magic and raise a holy army. Every upgrade makes your wizard and your troops stronger, while the battlefields grow larger and more dangerous.

Opposite you, the Necromancer King's legions, which also grow, and bosses to bring down.

That is all. And it is exactly what the genre asks for.

## What separates it from other horde games

One thing: **the army is yours**.

In most horde survival games, you are alone against the tide. Upgrades raise your damage, your rate of fire, your area. Power stays a statistic belonging to your character.

Here, you recruit holy troops and strengthen their ranks across the campaign. They fight beside you, they hold back the undead, and they add their power to every battle.

That changes two things.

**Your position matters.** You have to stand where your troops hold, and your troops hold where you put them. You are no longer a mobile dot, you are the centre of a line.

**And losing troops is a reproach.** In a solo game, losing health is an abstract penalty. Losing a unit you paid for and strengthened across three battles is something else.

The game asks you to combine your troops with elemental and arcane spells to break through legions that keep growing. That is a composition loop, not merely a stacking one.

![Legion Breaker, a battle](/images/legionbreaker/screenshot-2.webp)

## The skill tree

That is where your gold goes, and the game insists on one point worth noting: every upgrade creates a visible difference on the battlefield.

Attacks become stronger, cover more ground, destroy larger groups of enemies as your build develops.

That sounds obvious. It is not remotely obvious, and I had the demonstration this very week writing about another game in the genre, whose players complained precisely about never feeling their upgrades.

A progression game has to be visible. Explicitly promising that every purchase changes the picture on screen means understanding why people queue up again.

## The reservations, and they are honest

**Four reviews.** The game came out a few hours ago. I am not going to pretend I have a reading of its reception.

What is measurable is that **over seven hundred people are playing at once** on launch day, which is a lot for a game under six euros from a studio nobody knows.

**Depth is the unknown.** A tree described as massive says nothing about its branches. Do you choose between incompatible directions, or do you buy everything in whatever order you like? The difference between those is the entire replayability, and it does not show on a store page.

**And length.** At this price, nobody is promising you a hundred hours. If the game holds for ten or fifteen before repeating itself, the contract is fulfilled. Beyond that is a bonus.

![Legion Breaker, the Necromancer King](/images/legionbreaker/screenshot-3.webp)

What I think, off the cuff and owning it: this is an end-of-day game.

Not the one people will discuss in six months, not the one you recommend to somebody hunting their next great title. The one you launch at ten at night to kill skeletons for forty minutes while watching your army grow, and close without guilt.

There is room for that, and less of it than you would think at this price.
