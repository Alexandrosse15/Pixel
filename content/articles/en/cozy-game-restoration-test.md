The parcel was taped shut, I pulled the flaps apart one by one, and there was a gold cartridge inside. A real one, with the gold plastic shell publishers used to make in runs of fifty for competition winners.

Forty minutes of cotton buds, tweezers and toothbrush later, the game hands me my report card. Dirt 78%. Stickers 100%. Ink and adhesive 73%. Scratches 85%. Final grade: D.

And underneath, the line that made me laugh on my own: purchase price 22.32 dollars, market value 20.09 dollars, printed in red.

![A gold cartridge mid-clean, tweezers lifting a sticker off the label](/images/cozygamerestoration/screenshot-1.webp)

## The gesture, and why it works

You do not play the games. You clean them.

Every cartridge arrives procedurally filthy, and the filth is layered. There is dust, which brushes off. There are the car-boot-sale price stickers you have to lift by one corner with tweezers without tearing the original label underneath. There is the adhesive left behind once the sticker is gone, which needs a different tool. There is the green marker a kid from 1997 used to write his name across the fox on the cover of *Super Good Boys*. And there are the scratches, which never fully go.

The tool column on the left holds about ten. Finger, tweezers, cotton bud, toothbrush, brush, spray. None of them is good everywhere, each is perfect somewhere, and the game never tells you which to reach for.

That is where it works. The material responds: dirt smears if you scrub it dry, the sticker stretches before it gives, dust lifts in a small cloud. For twenty minutes I thought about nothing else, which is precisely what was advertised.

![A Super Good Boys cartridge covered in green marker, brushed over a wooden desk](/images/cozygamerestoration/screenshot-2.webp)

## The detail that saves it from tedium

A cleaning simulator with nothing at stake burns out in an hour. This one had a good idea, and it lives on the card that appears at the end.

Every restored game is graded the way real grading services do it: a letter, A to D. And it gets a full collection sheet. Platform (Nebula, Super 16, the game's invented consoles), region, year, genre, rarity, price paid, market value.

The 108 fictional titles are unapologetic pastiches of the 1980s to the early 2000s. *Fireball Spam + AirJumping III*, genre Fighting, 1998, published by SEGUL. *Story of Hats: Hourglass*. They are good jokes, and there are enough of them to keep curiosity alive: across a hundred-odd titles you start hunting for the rare ones, the regional variants, the gold shells.

So the cleaning stops being the point. You are not scrubbing to make something clean any more, you are scrubbing to wring a B out of a cartridge that did not deserve one. It is a collector's loop grafted onto a housework game, and it is smarter than it looks.

![The end-of-restoration screen: grade D, percentages per type of grime, collection sheet and market value in red](/images/cozygamerestoration/screenshot-4.webp)

## Where it drags

The hand holding the tools is soft. There is no better word for it: the precise gestures, lifting a sticker corner, tracking a groove, take a patience that the imprecise cursor sometimes makes irritating rather than meticulous. On a very dirty cartridge I caught myself scrubbing in random circles waiting for the percentage to climb.

And there is no way to fail, which is the stated principle, but it eventually drains the tension out of the last few dozen percent. You cannot botch a restoration, you can only rush it, and the game does not really hold it against you.

Then there is the question of length. After a dozen cartridges the gesture is learned and the collection is pulling the whole thing. If collectibles do nothing for you, the game stops there, around the three-hour mark.

![The card for a restored game with its fictional publisher label](/images/cozygamerestoration/screenshot-3.webp)

At around 15 euros, from a studio called Parasol Corp that went as far as printing its own logo on the certification stickers of its imaginary cartridges, the deal is fair. You are buying three hours of complete quiet and a handful of good finds.

What I will remember is the market value in red. Forty minutes of careful work for a two-dollar loss: there are collectors who will recognise their entire life in that screen.
