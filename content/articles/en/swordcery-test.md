Some premises make you want to see more immediately. Swordcery's: a giant blade has plunged into the world of Armorial, bringing a hail of smaller blades with it. That calamitous sword is draining the planet's energy, and with each passing day the rain of swords grows heavier.

It is visual, it is absurd, and above all it mechanically justifies everything else in the game. We like it when fiction and system hold hands.

![Swordcery, the action game and its rain of magical swords](/images/swordcery/screenshot-1.webp)

## One sword, one ability

You play Colt, a thoroughly unwilling hero who has gained the power to wield the fallen blades. Each new sword arrives with its own ability, its Swordcery: hurling your blade boomerang-style, thundering down as a bolt of lightning, opening hellfire fissures, or summoning a ring of protective icy swordflakes.

On top of that, every blade belongs to a Sword Class with its own set of offensive manoeuvres and combos. Depending on terrain and situation, one class proves more advantageous than another, and the studio Temple Door Games explicitly places mastery of these affinities at the centre of progression.

That is good architecture, because it separates two layers: the base combat style tied to the class, and the trick tied to the specific sword. Used well, that combinatorial approach avoids the action-game trap where every weapon ends up feeling the same.

## The player it is aimed at

The studio does not hide its intent and describes precisely what it expects from you: recognising enemy patterns, reacting to telegraphed attacks, alternating melee and magic, and managing resources in the heat of battle.

Translation: this is a game of reading and reflexes, not a power fantasy. Every encounter resolves by understanding your opponent before hitting them. We are in the family of demanding action games, with treacherous biomes and bosses built to test you.

## The unknowns

They matter and should be stated. A game built on weapon variety lives or dies on balance: if three blades dominate, the other fifty become scenery to collect. And an action game founded on reading attacks depends entirely on animation legibility and control responsiveness, two things no store page can prove.

The game launches today in Early Access, with no user reviews, which explains the absence of a score here. A demo is available and is clearly the right starting point. One practical caveat: it is offered in English only.

The idea is good and the setting is a delight. What remains is whether the sword is as pleasant to wield as it is to pick up.


The first players are unanimous at 100 percent positive, but across only about twenty reviews. That is excellent and entirely insufficient to conclude: this score needs confirming on a larger sample.
