Let's not kid ourselves, we're a little tired of football in video games. Every autumn, the same cake comes back, the same updated roster sold full price, the same feeling of pressing buttons while the game plays in our place. So when a small Belgian studio shows up with a football game entirely controlled by hand, free, and one that has already won over sixty thousand players in a demo, of course we prick up our ears. Strikers Club arrives on 17 July, and it's exactly the kind of kick in the anthill we were hoping for.

![Strikers Club, the physics football where every dribble and every shot are hand-controlled on the pitch](/images/strikersclub/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Strikers Club is a physics-driven multiplayer football simulation developed by Oddshot Games, a Belgian studio based in Ghent, already responsible for the likeable hockey game Slapshot: Rebound. The title launches on 17 July 2026 on Steam as full free-to-play, playable on keyboard as well as controller. And it's not a shot in the dark: during the last Steam Next Fest, the demo climbed into the top 50 most played, with more than sixty thousand players and half a million matches contested. In other words, the audience is already there, it has tested, and it wants more. For an independent production facing the genre's mastodons, that's a huge signal.

## What we know: skill, nothing but skill

The principle is clear and delightful: you control a single player on the pitch, and absolutely everything depends on your hands. You dribble to move while managing your direction and speed, you aim your shots with the camera, you chain skill moves to wipe out a defender with flair, you pass to launch a collective play. Here, no button-mashing that triggers a scripted animation: every second counts, every move is yours. Those who prefer to guard the net even get a goalie mode with its own dedicated mechanics, which promises real last-line heroes rather than an AI that just concedes.

Where Oddshot surprises is in its simulation ambition. The game embarks the full flow of a real match: throw-ins, corners, penalties, offside, fouls, the works. And it scales like no one else, from the nervy 1v1 duel on a smaller pitch to the full-size 11v11 that recreates the organized chaos of a real eleven-against-eleven. Add full player customization, jerseys, gloves, boots, accessories, and a Community Centre to practice through mini-games and chat with no pressure, and you get a surprisingly complete package for a game that costs nothing.

![Strikers Club, from the 1v1 duel to the full-size chaos of 11v11](/images/strikersclub/screenshot-2.webp)

## How it differs from Rematch

The comparison with Rematch, Sloclap's hit released last year, is inevitable, and that's exactly where Strikers Club asserts its personality. Rematch made a radical choice: pure arcade. Closed arenas with walls, a ball that never goes out, no offside, no fouls, no interruptions, from 3v3 to 5v5 like a supercharged futsal. It's a Rocket League with boots, snappy and with no downtime, and it's very good that way. Strikers Club takes the exact opposite path. Where Rematch erases the rules to keep only the flow, Strikers Club puts them all back, and it's a real football match it seeks to reproduce, with its set pieces, its offside to negotiate and its fouls to draw or avoid.

The other gap is scale. Rematch stays in the tight format, five players max per team, a futsal discipline where everything plays out fast and short. Strikers Club aims for the real pitch, up to eleven against eleven, with everything that implies of positioning, cover, width of play and real roles on the grass. Where Rematch is a street game raised to esport, Strikers Club wants to be the Sunday match transposed with no cheating. Two opposite philosophies of the same fantasy, and it's excellent news that they coexist: we'll no longer have to choose between arcade and simulation, we'll have both.

## Why we're glad to escape FIFA

Because let's be frank, football in video games was going in circles. The annual ogre sells us the same thing every year at full price, a simulation where your team plays largely on its own, where player stats and wallet thickness often count more than your talent, and where the bulk of the move goes through contextual animations you trigger without really executing them. We ended up confusing watching with playing. Strikers Club flips the table by putting skill back at the center: here, you can steal nothing through button-mashing, victory depends on your ability, your reading of the game and your understanding with flesh-and-blood teammates.

Add the sinews of war, total free-to-play, and you understand our enthusiasm. A football game that doesn't ask seventy euros for a jersey update, that bets on the pleasure of the move rather than the credit card, and that treats its players as athletes to develop rather than consumers to milk, is precisely the breath of air we were demanding. Strikers Club doesn't seek to dethrone the giant on its own ground, it offers something else, and that something else appeals to us.

![Strikers Club, the goalie mode and its dedicated mechanics for real last-line heroes](/images/strikersclub/screenshot-3.webp)

## What worries us

We're still keeping a cool head on two points. The first is the business model. Free-to-play is a magnificent promise as long as it stays honest, but the announcement of a Pro offer with tokens, cosmetics and a growing list of benefits calls for vigilance: it'll all depend on the line between harmless cosmetics and advantage you pay for. The second is how the 11v11 holds up with strangers. An eleven-against-eleven where each player controls only one footballer can turn into a total mess if the coordination or the netcode don't follow, and that's precisely where the game will play out its long-term credibility.

## We'll be there, whistle in hand

None of this dents our impatience. Strikers Club ticks a rare number of good boxes: a real gameplay idea, an execution already validated by tens of thousands of players, a philosophy that puts talent back at the center and a zero-euro entry ticket that's just a click away. Facing a genre sclerotic with habit and a Rematch that opened the arcade path, it offers the simulation version, ambitious and generous, of the same dream. We'll be on the pitch from 17 July, and for once a football game genuinely excites us, we won't sulk our pleasure.
