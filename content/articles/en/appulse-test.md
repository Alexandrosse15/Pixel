Appulse's first ten minutes are disorienting. You bring pinball reflexes: wait for the ball, flip at the right moment, pray. Then you realise the ball is a small armed ship, and every shot pushes it the opposite way. From then on, you are not playing pinball any more. You are piloting.

A shot downwards, and the ball climbs instead of slipping between the flippers. A sideways burst, and it corrects course at the last moment. A well-measured string of shots, and it hangs above a swarm of enemies long enough to clear them all. It is a gesture that did not exist before, and Le Rado made it instantly readable. We described it in [our preview](/articles/appulse-preview); in hand, it delivers on every promise.

![A table mid-battle, red neon and explosions](/images/appulse/screenshot-5.webp)

The roguelite around it is classic, which is both its limit and its strength. You defend the Vektor Dynamics fleet against the Iron Accord, advance across a galaxy map, and pick upgrades for the ball: more recoil, harder impacts, slow motion to chain combos. Tables change, enemies too, and each run pushes a little further. Nothing revolutionary in the structure, but the core gesture is so satisfying it does not need to be.

What I liked most is that luck almost disappears. In classic pinball, a ball lost between the flippers is often down to chance. Here, it is nearly always on you: you did not fire, or not at the right time. The studio keeps saying "no luck, just skill", and it is true.

![Choosing a shuttle in the middle of a table](/images/appulse/screenshot-2.webp)

Readability sometimes suffers when the screen fills with neon, projectiles and score numbers, and the tables end up looking a bit alike in their dark red aesthetic. Alain Girardot's soundtrack carries the tension very well, to the point of being sold separately.

At 12.99 euros (20% off for two weeks), Steam Deck Verified, and entirely handmade as the studio proudly states, it is one of the freshest ideas of the autumn. Raw Fury has the eye, once again.
