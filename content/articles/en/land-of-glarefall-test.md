6.93 euros.

I am starting there because it changes the conversation entirely. At sixty euros, Land of Glarefall would be a game you take apart in three paragraphs. At seven it is something else: a reasonable bet on a simple idea executed cleanly.

![Land of Glarefall, exploring by day](/images/glarefall/screenshot-1.webp)

You are a royal investigator. You are sent to look into an energy source called Black Gold, and into the creatures it attracts. The setting is laid out in three sentences and the game does not come back to it much, which is a decision rather than an oversight.

Because the real subject is the clock.

By day you explore, you gather wood and ore, you build, you push the story along. By night, waves of monsters arrive. There is no third option. Everything you do between sunrise and sunset is an answer to one question: will I be ready.

It is a structure as old as the hills and I still find it brutal, because it turns every exploration decision into arithmetic. That ore vein is a four-minute walk away. There are six minutes of daylight left. You go, or you head back and reinforce the palisade. You already know what you are going to do, and you already know you will regret it.

## Heroes and mercenaries

Two systems carry the runtime.

The first is the heroes. Several characters, each with their own skill tree, pushed towards frontline aggression or tactical support. Nothing revolutionary, but the trees are different enough to justify a second run, and that is all anyone asks of them.

The second is more interesting: the outposts. You build them, and they let you hire and upgrade mercenaries who fight alongside you and hold the line during the night assaults. That moves the problem. In a standard survival game, putting up a wall is enough. Here, a wall with nobody behind it only slows things down. So you have to arbitrate between fortifying and paying people, and that is richer than it has any right to be at seven euros.

![Land of Glarefall, a night assault](/images/glarefall/screenshot-2.webp)

## Where it cracks

The combat is functional and no more. Blows lack weight, enemy readability wobbles when a night gets genuinely busy, and there are moments where you win because you had more mercenaries, not because you played better.

The art direction holds up without ever surprising you. This is the usual visual vocabulary of the genre, dark forests and blue glows, and you wish Black Gold had a stronger look of its own, given that it is supposed to be the centre of this world.

And the game is small. Not sloppy, small. There is an end to the content, it arrives sooner than in the big names of the genre, and you should know that before you click.

![Land of Glarefall, building an outpost](/images/glarefall/screenshot-3.webp)

So far the reception follows: 40 positive reviews out of 42, and a few hundred players online at once as I write. A modest, present community, which is exactly the profile you would expect from a title this size.

![Land of Glarefall, the creatures drawn to Black Gold](/images/glarefall/screenshot-4.webp)

What I really liked was the moment I realised I had spent my money wrong. I had three outposts and nobody in them, because I had put everything into walls. The night punished me cleanly, with no error message, simply by walking through. This is a game that teaches by consequence, and at this price that is already a lot.
