Last week I wrote that an idle game wedged along the bottom of your desktop had thirty-four thousand people playing at once. That was [Deskrawl](/en/articles/deskrawl-test).

Today another one arrives, built on exactly the same idea: **a game that lives in your taskbar**.

Two in a fortnight is no longer a coincidence, it is a format taking hold. And honestly, I understand why.

![Duelist: Idle Expedition, the guild on expedition](/images/duelistidle/screenshot-1.webp)

Out last night, it stands at **70 positive reviews out of 80, or 87 per cent**, with more than a thousand people playing at once.

## What it is

An automatic battler with JRPG pixel art, where your guild grows on its own. The studio presents it as the best companion to keep you company during your working days, which is a sentence of disarming frankness.

And the announced depth is genuinely unusual for the genre.

**Seven main classes, twenty-one sub-classes, and ninety-one distinct roles in total.** Every duelist has their own abilities and animations.

Ninety-one. In a game that runs in the background.

That figure says something important: this is not a game where you watch a bar fill, it is a team composition game. The work is not clicking, it is choosing who goes on expedition with whom.

## The equipment

Over two hundred pieces to collect across the adventure, over sixty legendary items locked to specific classes, and an enchanting system to tune each piece.

That is the classic loot loop, transposed into a format where you are not there to watch it drop. Which changes everything: your pleasure does not come from the moment the item appears, it comes from the moment you open the tab and find out what the day produced.

It is exactly what I described about Deskrawl, and it is the real appeal of this format for people who are thirty-six and have a calendar.

![Duelist: Idle Expedition, roles and classes](/images/duelistidle/screenshot-2.webp)

## The camp, and the best line on the store page

You also run a camp, with facilities to upgrade.

**The tavern** is where you recruit new talent. And, I quote, where you **earn revenue from their wine consumption**.

I read it three times. You recruit adventurers, you send them off to risk their lives across the continent, and while they rest you sell them drinks and take the money.

That is the most cynically realistic mechanic I have read all week, and I love it.

**The blacksmith** equips your squad and produces extra goods for the camp's commerce. **The shopkeeper** moves your spoils to fund the next trip.

In other words, there is a camp economy sitting on top of the automatic combat, and it is what turns an idle game into a management game.

![Duelist: Idle Expedition, the camp](/images/duelistidle/screenshot-3.webp)

## The two questions

**Are the ninety-one roles genuinely different?** That is the whole question. If they amount to shifted statistics, the game becomes a spreadsheet with sprites. If they have behaviours that answer each other, then there is a real team-building space, and that is what brings you back daily for three months.

**And the monetisation.** The price was not displayed as I write. In an offline progression game with collectible equipment and enchanting, that is where everything is decided: selling time is the easiest thing in the world, and it is the trap this genre lays for all its authors.

I am accusing nobody. I am simply saying it is the first thing I will look at, as with every game in this format.

![Duelist: Idle Expedition, the equipment](/images/duelistidle/screenshot-4.webp)

What I like about this wave is that it owns something the industry does not enjoy saying: most people no longer have three uninterrupted hours. A game that acknowledges that and builds around it, rather than guilting you with a daily login, provides a real service.

Early feedback leans the right way, and the question of the ninety-one roles will be settled in a month, not in an evening.
