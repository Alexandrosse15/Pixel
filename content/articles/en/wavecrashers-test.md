Take a MOBA. Keep the heroes, the items bought during the match, the synergies that reveal themselves over the minutes, the builds that fork.

Now remove the opposing team.

What is left is Wavecrashers, and it is cleverer than it sounds.

![Wavecrashers, defending the core](/images/wavecrashers/screenshot-1.webp)

## The structure

Hordes of enemies spawn in waves to destroy a core building. Three players team up to stop them and must endure to the final wave to win the round.

That is lane defence in its barest form. No surprises in the description.

The surprise is in what happens during.

Every hero is built around a distinct combat identity, but none is locked into a single role. You shape them through talents, perks and powerful items, and the studio says it plainly: builds can bend, or completely redefine, a character's intended playstyle.

And as in a MOBA, heroes keep evolving throughout the battle. You purchase items, strengthen key attributes, unlock synergies, adapt your build to the problems arriving.

That is the real idea. Most wave defence games have you choose your build before you start and then watch the result. Here you build while it burns, and a build that answered wave 6 perfectly can be the wrong build at wave 14.

## The Tides, or the part that makes you go again

When the normal battle is no longer enough, you descend into the Tides.

It is an escalating series of challenges that steadily reshapes the rules of the game. Each tier adds harsher conditions, stronger enemies, tighter preparation windows, empowered bosses, disruptive effects. Beating a tier unlocks the next, and the pressure stacks onto future battles.

Anyone who has pushed greater rifts in an action RPG, or ascension levels in a card game, recognises the mechanic. It works because it turns difficulty into content: you are not replaying the same level harder, you are replaying a level whose rules have changed, and your build has to change with them.

![Wavecrashers, a hero's talents and items](/images/wavecrashers/screenshot-2.webp)

## The Ordeals

And on top of that sits the thing I like best.

Every Tide run throws up Ordeals: dangerous choices that emerge during battle and force you to decide how the struggle will evolve. The higher you climb, the more severe the available Ordeals become.

A choice that lands mid-fight, while the wave is coming through, while your two teammates wait for you to confirm. That is a kind of tension co-op games rarely achieve, because the decision is usually taken in a menu, calmly, beforehand.

Taking a voluntary aggravation in exchange for an advantage, three-handed, with no time to discuss, is an excellent generator of post-match arguments.

![Wavecrashers, a wave of enemies incoming](/images/wavecrashers/screenshot-3.webp)

## What to know going in

**This is a three-player game.** Not two, not five. The whole wave economy and the map layout rest on it. If you normally play in a pair, you will be sitting in a queue every session.

**This is day one of early access.** The studio leads with the entirely positive reviews of its demo, which is a marketing number rather than a reception, since a demo attracts exactly the people who already like the genre. The real test starts now.

**And the hero count is the thing to watch.** A game whose promise is build variety lives or dies on its roster. At launch you get through the available identities fairly quickly, and the depth then comes purely from talents and items. That holds for a few dozen hours, not indefinitely, and it is precisely what early access will have to fix.

![Wavecrashers, an empowered boss in the Tides](/images/wavecrashers/screenshot-4.webp)

My memory of the week: a wave 15 where I had built a character entirely around a synergy that never once triggered, because an Ordeal a teammate accepted had changed how the enemies behaved. I was useless for three minutes. I was furious, and I queued again immediately.

That is the tell that never lies.
