Start with the number, because it is rare.

**1,023 reviews, 97 per cent positive.** Overwhelmingly positive. For a shop management game released three days ago at 17.99 euros, in a genre drowning in slapdash productions.

One player says so outright: this is not like the flood of low-effort shop sims on Steam.

![Probably Stolen, the shop](/images/probablystolen/screenshot-1.webp)

## The situation

In a towering space station split by wealth and corruption, your business runs on merchandise with flexible origins.

You are a former scavenger who secured a storefront thanks to a generous loan and questionable timing. Rent is rising, factions are watching, and most of your best margins live in legal grey zones.

And the central point: **you never really know who is on the other side of the counter.**

Today's customer could be a security officer fishing for evidence, a revolutionary seeking leverage, or a fallen elite testing your usefulness.

Every sale is a gamble. You approve it, you refuse it, you report it, or you look away. Someone is always taking notes.

## Why it sits above the rest of the genre

Because the sentence closing its pitch is a genuine design thesis.

**"You're not choosing a theme for your shop, you're choosing which kind of trouble you're willing to take on."**

You start as a gunsmith, chemist, water merchant, fence, farmer, or from absolute rock bottom. And every product line has its own profit curve **and its own way of ruining you**: weapons bring inspections, chemicals bring addiction, water brings liability, contraband brings raids.

Some trades need heavy machinery and permits, others just a good hiding spot and a straight face.

The better the margins, the worse the consequences when something goes wrong.

That is an economic system with morality built in, and it is precisely what nearly every retail simulator lacks. Selling is never neutral here.

![Probably Stolen, the customers](/images/probablystolen/screenshot-2.webp)

## Reputation as a double-sided resource

The studio puts it well: reputation buys access and protection, but it also paints a target on your back.

That is the mechanic stopping the game from resolving into optimisation. In a conventional simulator, the more you succeed, the safer you are. Here, the more you succeed, the more people watch.

## What the players say

The game's best review is an anecdote, which is always a good sign.

**"Hid my storage chest with my most expensive stuff in the trashcan during a surprise police inspection and forgot to take it out. Garbage collector becomes richest man overnight. 10/10."**

Another warns: if you are prone to saying "just one more turn", this game is a time machine that only goes forwards.

And a third, with forty-nine hours of playtest and nearly nineteen hours of demo logged before even buying, simply says he cannot recommend it any higher.

![Probably Stolen, the space station](/images/probablystolen/screenshot-3.webp)

## The reservations, and they are thin

**A thousand reviews is still young.** A 97 per cent reception three days after release largely reflects an audience that already knew the game from the demo and playtest. The real test is next month, when the people who had never heard of it arrive.

**And this is a dense management game.** Several players talk about heavily fleshed out mechanics. That is a compliment for anyone wanting depth, and a warning for anyone who wanted a relaxing shop game to launch in the evening.

![Probably Stolen, the merchandise](/images/probablystolen/screenshot-4.webp)

What stays with me is the intelligence of the framing.

Putting a shop in a corrupt space station gives dramatic weight to a banal act. Selling a bottle of water becomes a political decision when water is rationed and the buyer might be an informant.

There have been around thirty retail simulators this year. This is the only one I will remember, because it understood the interest was never in the till but in the person opposite.
