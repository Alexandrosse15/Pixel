The medieval automation game has become a crowded sub-genre, and most candidates settle for repainting Factorio in fantasy.

Alchemy Factory does something else, and it comes down to one decision: **your factory does not have to stay on a single floor**.

![Alchemy Factory, the workshop and its conveyors](/images/alchemyfactory/screenshot-1.webp)

## The basic action

You start with a small shop. You grind, extract, distil, smelt, process.

Conveyors connect everything, permanently, and pipes carry the liquids. You chain devices into complete production lines, then spend the rest of your life balancing inputs against outputs and hunting bottlenecks.

It is the genre's classic loop, and it is unusually legible here.

## The idea that changes things: verticality

You place conveyors, pipes, machines and your shop freely, in every direction, **and at every height**.

In practice a production chain can climb a floor and come back down further along, and scattered functional zones end up integrating into a compact network instead of sprawling across an infinite floor.

That has two effects. The first is aesthetic: a vertical factory is something you look at, whereas a flat one is something you fly over. The second is strategic: your constraint is no longer available surface, it is your ability to think in three dimensions, and that is markedly harder.

![Alchemy Factory, a multi-level factory](/images/alchemyfactory/screenshot-3.webp)

## And there is a shop at the end

That is what stops the game spinning in a vacuum.

You are not producing for the sake of producing, you are filling shelves. Potions, jewellery, relics. You serve customers, you complete commissions, and once sales are automated you can go back to what actually interests you: expanding.

Money buys land, research and equipment upgrades, which raises capacity, which raises sales. The circle is virtuous and perfectly calibrated to cost you an evening.

## In co-op, and it is well judged

The game plays with others, online and over local network.

The studio describes exactly what I see in practice: in a shared factory the roles divide themselves. Somebody optimises lines, somebody handles management, somebody designs the space. Building upwards helps a great deal, because two people can work on two different floors without treading on each other.

There is Steam Workshop too, for whatever the community wants to add.

![Alchemy Factory, the shop and its shelves](/images/alchemyfactory/screenshot-5.webp)

## The reception, which is clear

The game launched yesterday and already has **2,321 reviews, 2,064 of them positive**, close to nine satisfied players out of ten.

That is not a curiosity figure, it is a usage figure, and at that volume it means something: people who bought a medieval automation game at 13.99 euros got what they came for.

On hardware, expect a GTX 1060 and eight gigabytes of memory, which stays reasonable.

## What to know

The genre has its burdens and they apply here. The first hour is laborious while you learn the machines' vocabulary. The curve demands patience. And if factories bore you, medieval paint will not change that.

But if you are one of those people who opens an automation game telling themselves they will just fix one small detail before bed, this one has a dangerous quirk: your factories are pretty. You will want to show them off, so you will want them presentable, so you will rebuild them.
