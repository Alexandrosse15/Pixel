There's a little voice, in every extraction game, still whispering one more chest, one more room, you've got time. That voice is what kills you. Dwarf Delve made it the very heart of its gameplay: here, greed isn't a flaw, it's a survival mechanic. Every meter dug toward the depths promises better loot, but brings you closer to the moment the elevator leaves without you. That constant dilemma, that tug-of-war between avarice and caution, is terribly simple on paper and devilishly addictive controller in hand.

![Dwarf Delve, the mining extraction roguelite where digging deeper promises more loot and more danger](/images/dwarfdelve/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Dwarf Delve is a first-person extraction roguelite developed by solo studio Gloom Box and published by indie.io, available on PC since 27 July 2026. The principle: you play a Delveling who descends into procedurally generated mines to gather materials, with the goal of getting back to the surface before time runs out. Each expedition offers different routes, dangers and deposits, and the deepest sections hold the most valuable materials, but lengthen the trip back accordingly. Collapsing tunnels, traps and underground hazards can end a run and make you lose all the loot you've gathered. A tense extraction loop, backed by permanent upgrades.

## The gameplay: greed as the engine

The heart of Dwarf Delve is its extraction dilemma, and it's brilliant in its simplicity. You descend, you mine, you gather, and at every moment the same question arises: do I go back up now with what I have, or push deeper for juicier loot, at the risk of missing the elevator back? The depths brim with precious materials, but the longer you linger, the more the danger grows and the longer the return path becomes. That risk-and-reward tension, where your worst enemy is your own greed, is the game's engine, and it works beautifully: you miss an elevator for one last vein, you lose everything, and you relaunch right away swearing you'll be more reasonable next time.

The tools and equipment enrich that loop with real flavor. The Delveling wields a Battlemallet to break rock and confront hazards, and leans on a whole arsenal of caving tools: lanterns, flares, support beams, ladders and floodlights, so many items that turn the mine into terrain to develop. You can even build rail networks and use minecarts to transport heavy ores through the tunnels, adding a welcome dimension of logistical organization. The resources brought back to the surface are refined and exchanged for permanent upgrades, new tools, skills and perks, increasing movement speed, mining strength and carrying capacity. Each successful run durably strengthens the next, in the progression spiral specific to the roguelite.

![Dwarf Delve, rails, minecarts and a caving arsenal turn the mine into terrain to develop](/images/dwarfdelve/screenshot-2.webp)

## What sticks

You have to stay lucid about the limits of the genre and the project. The extraction roguelite is an addictive but repetitive formula by nature, where you redo largely the same loop with each descent, betting on better loot. Whoever doesn't take to the risk-and-reward principle, to that tension of punished greed, won't change their mind here. Losing all your loot on failure, the engine of the tension, can also frustrate players allergic to punishment, and you have to love starting over and learning from your mistakes to get your money's worth over time.

The other reservation is its status as a just-released solo-developer game. A roguelite's richness depends on the variety of its mines, hazards, bestiary and the depth of its upgrade tree, and it'll take hindsight to know whether Dwarf Delve goes the distance or runs out of steam once the mechanics are mastered and the main upgrades unlocked. Procedural generation guarantees a certain renewal, but it doesn't replace the diversity of handcrafted content. It's a title to approach while supporting an independent creator, betting on its evolution as much as on its launch state, while enjoying an already fearsomely effective loop.

![Dwarf Delve, refined resources are exchanged for permanent upgrades](/images/dwarfdelve/screenshot-3.webp)

## What we take away

Dwarf Delve is a little success resting on a simple but brilliant idea: making greed a survival mechanic. Its extraction dilemma, that constant tug-of-war between pushing deeper for better loot and getting back up before it's too late, is terribly addictive, and it's enriched by a tasty arsenal of tools, rails to build and permanent progression that gives meaning to each descent. For a solo-developer game, the loop is already remarkably effective, and you catch yourself chaining runs, swearing each time to be more reasonable.

You just have to love the genre and accept its limits. The extraction roguelite is repetitive by nature, its punishment can frustrate, and its status as a brand-new solo game leaves questions of variety and longevity to confirm over the updates. But on the essential, the risk-and-reward tension and the pleasure of digging ever deeper, Dwarf Delve delivers exactly what it promises, with a personality and efficiency that make it hard to put down. An underground gem for whoever loves playing with their own greed, and losing because of it.

## Verdict

A first-person mining extraction roguelite that makes greed a survival mechanic, with an addictive extraction dilemma and a tasty arsenal: fearsomely gripping, with the reservations of a just-released solo game.

**Strengths:**

- The extraction dilemma, addictive, where greed is your worst enemy
- A tasty arsenal, from the Battlemallet to rails and minecarts
- Permanent progression that gives meaning to each descent
- Procedural mines that renew the expeditions

**Weaknesses:**

- A loop repetitive by nature, specific to the genre
- Total loot loss that will frustrate those allergic to punishment
- A solo-developer game with variety and longevity to confirm
