There is a line in this game's pitch that made me smile, because it lies outright: **no pressure.**

It comes immediately after explaining that an invasion is coming, that each wave is stronger than the last, and that all these people want one thing, the destruction of your Powercore. The sense of humour is there.

![Lorne, the bastion and its fortifications](/images/lorne/screenshot-1.webp)

## How it works

The game lives in a tension between two contradictory urges, and that is what makes it good.

**You have to go out**, because everything you build comes from outside. Every structure starts with an expedition: you gather materials, bring them home, craft. The world of Lorne also hides ancient artefacts that unlock new powers and technologies, and soul fragments that upgrade abilities.

**You have to stay**, because the clock starts ticking the moment the run begins and the rampart does not build itself.

That is a good dilemma. Every minute spent exploring is a minute the defence does not progress, and every artefact left out there is power you are denying yourself. You push the return back one more room, you hear the wave coming, you run.

On the defensive side, you design your own fortifications, upgrade them, adapt the layout. The attackers vary: plantlings, evil mages, stumplings, elementals, hordes of undead.

## The role you play

The framing is better held than average. You are not an anonymous survivor, you are a **ranger**, with a defined function in the world: investigating conflicts, uncovering mysteries, finding ways to bring down dangerous mythical creatures, guarding the borders and roaming the land to keep the peace.

It is a writing detail, but it does a lot. A defence game where you are simply a man with a hammer tells you nothing. A game where you have a duty gives you a reason to start again.

![Lorne, exploration and gathering](/images/lorne/screenshot-2.webp)

## What I liked and what worries me

What works is the blend. Pure tower defence turns abstract fast, pure exploration turns limp fast. Making them depend on each other, with a countdown in the middle, creates a natural rhythm: calm phase, panic phase, breather, again.

Adding artefacts is clever because it gives exploration an objective that is not merely wood. You go out looking for power, not logs.

What worries me is structural, and it is the classic risk of this type of game.

**Repetition.** If the waves only get bigger, strategy freezes. A player finds the wall layout that works and reproduces it to the end. Longevity will depend entirely on the real variety of attackers: undead and elementals must demand different defences, otherwise the game becomes an exercise in stacking.

**And the countdown's balance.** Too short and you never explore, making the world pointless. Too long and the threat vanishes, leaving only a building game. Everything turns on that setting, and it is precisely the sort of thing early access spends months adjusting.

The game launches today, made by Lorne Studios, with no player reviews yet.

![Lorne, a wave of attackers at the ramparts](/images/lorne/screenshot-3.webp)

## What it is worth

Six and a half.

In its favour: a tension between leaving and staying that structures playtime very well, artefacts that give exploration a stake beyond logistics, fortifications you design rather than place, and a ranger role that gives all of it a reason to exist.

Against it: early access in a genre where repetition is enemy number one, and a balance between exploration and defence that will decide everything without anyone yet being able to judge it.

It is a good skeleton. We will see how long it stays standing.
