There was a precise moment when I realised this game was not playing in the same category as its rivals.

It was seeing the term **available rotation hours** appear in a monthly report. Then EBITDA. In a ten-euro airline game made by one person.

A Chinese player's Steam review sums it up better than I can: an aviation enthusiast who has worked through Airline Tycoon 2, the Kairosoft games, Airport Tycoon, Airlines Manager and AM4, and who says he had to go and look up some of the terminology. He recommends it anyway.

That is exactly the right warning to put at the top of a review.

![Airline Founder, the world map and route network](/images/airlinefounder/screenshot-1.webp)

## What it brings over the usual sims

Since that is the real question, let us take it point by point.

**The size of the database.** The game is built from public data: roughly **eight thousand airports, 7,560 cities and over 36,000 seed routes**. This is not a fictional map or a selection of thirty famous airports, it is the global network. You are not choosing between Paris and New York, you are choosing between thousands of city pairs whose demand you have to compare.

**The constraints others skip.** Airport capacity, runway length, slot pressure, **night curfews and noise limits**. In most games of the genre, opening a route means clicking a destination and picking an aircraft. Here you have to check the runway takes your aircraft, that a slot remains, and that the airport does not close overnight exactly when your rotation was due to land.

**Corporate finance as a gameplay layer.** This is the real difference. Most simulators stop at profitability per route. This one takes you through to debt, leverage, **preparing an IPO**, and a share price that reacts to revenue, profit, leverage, reputation and events. Early access adds stock investments, IPO financing, subsidiaries and corporate groups.

**Reputation as seen by those watching you.** A fictional social media system has passengers, spotters, employees and investors react to your fares, punctuality, connections and safety record. It is dressing, but it gives legible consequences to decisions that would otherwise stay as numbers.

**The business model.** 10.25 euros once, no subscription. The online references in this genre live on monthly fees or shops, and that is not a detail for a game you keep open for six months.

![Airline Founder, fleet management](/images/airlinefounder/screenshot-3.webp)

## The loop, concretely

You start from a base airport, with a scenario of your choice, Tokyo, Singapore or Paris, and a strategy to commit to: full-service carrier, low-cost scale, regional network, or long-haul premium.

After that, everything is a trade-off.

You **buy, lease or hunt** for aircraft on a used market, track their condition, maintain them, reconfigure cabins. You set fares, service tiers, catering and frequencies. You build a hub-and-spoke network around your bases.

And above all that, the market moves without you: fuel shocks, regional travel booms, airport fee changes, maintenance directives, demand downturns.

It is an optimisation tunnel, in the most literal sense. You open a route, adjust a fare, read the monthly report, and start again.

## The developer saw the problem coming

A simulator this dense has a native flaw: it drowns newcomers.

The developer, working alone at Nerowan, heard that and answered with two things I find remarkable for a one-person team.

First, a **fully customisable interface**: you resize a button, rearrange the layout of an entire page, choose the colours and the information displayed.

Second, and cleverer still, an **information density setting**. Lower it and the game shows less detail and uses fewer technical terms. In other words, you can ask the game to stop talking like an annual report while you learn.

Few simulators of this kind accept that they have an accessibility problem. This one shipped a slider for it.

## What grates today, and it has to be said

The game launched yesterday into early access, and the first reviews are **evenly split, four positive and four negative out of eight**. That is far too few to draw a rating from, but the complaints are precise and consistent.

**The cost of airport access.** Several players describe the same thing: without buying access rights at the destination airport, the return flight goes back empty. And those rights cost millions, sometimes more than an aircraft, including at regional airports. As a result, starting a regional network becomes very hard without comfortable starting cash. That is a balance problem rather than a design one, but it hits precisely the first hour of play.

**Fuel economics.** One player with six hours logged describes routes earning several million whose costs swallow half of it after a jet fuel price rise, on easy difficulty. Realism is one thing, permanent punishment is another.

**The legibility of causes.** This is the most interesting complaint, and it comes from a positive review: you cannot clearly see the link between a route's passenger loading and the adjustments you just made. In an optimisation game, not knowing which decision produced which effect is a serious flaw, because it turns strategy into guesswork.

**And launch bugs.** Saves that fail, aircraft sales at the recommended price that do not go through, panels resetting on the daily data refresh, translation still rough in places.

One last point to clarify, because the store page contradicts itself: the description calls it a **single-player** simulation, while the page announces multiplayer, online co-op, Workshop and a level editor, added at the early access launch. It is the sales copy that has not kept up.

![Airline Founder, slots and flight scheduling](/images/airlinefounder/screenshot-7.webp)

## So who is it for

If you want an airline game to play in the evening without thinking, this is not it, and it never will be.

If, on the other hand, you are the sort to open a spreadsheet to compare two pricing strategies, this game was written for you, and it costs the price of a takeaway. The trade-off is that it is young, unbalanced in places, and will lose you routes for reasons you will not immediately understand.

The developer publishes patch notes weekly and answers feedback, which is the best available indicator on an early access. My recommendation is simple: try the demo first, it exists, and half an hour will tell you whether the information density stimulates you or flattens you.
