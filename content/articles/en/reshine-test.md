There is a diving helmet in Reshine. An old copper helmet, tarnished and dented, sitting on the attic workbench while snow falls outside the window. You take it apart porthole by porthole, screw by screw. You clean it, sand it, remove the oxidation. You pick a finish. You put it back together. And the game shows you the before and after.

That is all. And it is exactly what it needed to be.

![A restored diving helmet at the centre of the workbench](/images/reshine/screenshot-7.webp)

In [our preview](/articles/reshine-preview), we highlighted Quite OK Games' promise: no shop, no customers, no skill tree, no meta progression. The final version keeps it. Reshine is a pure game of calm, with no way to fail, no timer, where each object is a small session of focus.

The variety of objects is what makes it. A tin box, a needle multimeter, a ship's compass in its binnacle, with a short historical note. Each object has its own disassembly logic, and the more complex ones require you to really understand how the parts fit together. The studio estimates the whole thing at 8 to 10 hours.

![A restored ship's compass and its historical note](/images/reshine/screenshot-8.webp)

You can criticise the game for what it proudly claims: once an object is finished, there is nothing to do but move on to the next or redo it in other colours. Those who need a goal or progression will get bored quickly. The twelve cutting mats to unlock do not change much.

But for what it is, Reshine is unusually sincere. The studio, three people who came from a demanding city-builder, insists in its latest announcement that every asset and every line of code was made by hand. Kevin "Kounine" Colombin's soundtrack, with real saxophone recordings, is a perfect match for the rain and snow.

![A needle multimeter on the workbench](/images/reshine/screenshot-6.webp)

At 8.99 euros, with 20% off at launch, it is a gift for anyone who loves restoration videos and slow afternoons.
