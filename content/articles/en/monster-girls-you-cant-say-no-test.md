Let us start with the only warning that matters: this game is pornographic. Not suggestive, not saucy, not "mature audiences" in the sense we use about a war film. Pornographic, with the Steam content descriptors to match, and exactly one image in this article because it is the only one I can decently publish.

![Monster Girls: You Can't Say No, the Steam store artwork](/images/monstergirls/screenshot-1.webp)

## Why cover it at all

Because it is a game sold on Steam, released on 15 August 2026, and pretending that part of the catalogue does not exist would be an odd way to cover video games. Steam has hosted this category since 2018, it represents serious commercial volume, and treating it as a blind spot says more about embarrassment than about editorial policy.

And because the numbers are interesting. The game shows **81 positive reviews out of 84**, a Very Positive reception. It is still a small sample, but a ratio like that does not happen by accident in two days.

The studio is called Lewd Star, the name is evidently not trying to fool anybody, and the game sells for around eight euros.

## What it actually is

Steam files it under adventure, casual and simulation, which is a polite way of saying visual novel with choices. You play a protagonist facing a cast of fantasy-inspired female creatures, and the title lays out the programme clearly enough.

The content descriptors declared by the developer are explicit: explicit depictions of sexual situations, revealing outfits, nudity, animated sex scenes, BDSM and masturbation. There is no ambiguity, no content hidden behind a patch, no workaround strategy. The game announces exactly what it is.

That is precisely what deserves noting, because it is not the norm in this category. Part of this market runs on vagueness: a coy store page, a Discord link to the real content, a censored build you uncensor elsewhere. Here the contract is on the table. You know what you are buying, the description does not lie, and in a genre where impulse purchase is the dominant business model, that candour has real value.

## The actual subject, which is not the sex

The game is available in French, in a list of languages running past twenty. For eight euros, that is a localisation effort beyond what far better funded studios manage, and it is the kind of detail that explains a 96 percent rating much better than the content does.

Because here is what that reception really says: in this category, players are not rating graphical performance or narrative depth. They are rating whether the product matches its promise and whether it is finished. An adult game that crashes, that cuts off halfway, or whose advertised content half arrives in some hypothetical update, gets demolished without mercy. This one has not been, which suggests it delivers what it advertises.

## What sets the score

It marks down, first, what cannot be shown. A game with no image publishable in a general outlet forfeits all visual word of mouth, and I would add that it is hard to assess formal qualities when the entire promotional package reduces to its selling point.

It accounts for the fact that a catalogue of scenes is not a game structure. We are in direct consumption here rather than progression, and nobody pretends otherwise, the studio included.

It rewards the candour of the commercial contract, the generous localisation for the price, and a reception I have no reason to dispute even though it rests on a small sample.

A practical plus, the game is available in French.
