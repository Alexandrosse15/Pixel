The extraction shooter genre has found its recipe, its figures and its heavyweights. Tarkov laid the hardcore foundations, ARC Raiders recently proved the formula could be made accessible and clean at last, and a swarm of pretenders is pushing behind. Enginefall arrives with an idea no one had dared: what if the battlefield weren't a map, but a colossal train you climb from the tail to first class? On paper, it's genius. In the demo, it's already more complicated.

![Enginefall, the car-by-car climb of a post-apocalyptic megatrain](/images/enginefall/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Enginefall is a crafting-focused extraction shooter developed by Red Rover Interactive, expected on PC and Linux around late 2026 or 2027. The game is currently running a public demo, after a playtest the studio eventually rebranded as a demo mid-course, a detail we'll come back to. The pitch is immediately appealing: on post-apocalyptic megatrains, you start alone or with friends in the slum-cars of the tail, you loot, you craft, you build, and you climb the convoy car by car up to first class. Every carriage is there to loot, to defend, to turn into a base or to claim.

## What Enginefall brings against ARC Raiders

Compared to ARC Raiders, which built its success on a legible open map and a PvPvE loop of exemplary cleanness, Enginefall radically changes spatial scale. Where ARC Raiders drops you into a vast exterior, Enginefall locks you into a moving corridor. The megatrain is a linear space, vertical in its social progression, a playable Snowpiercer: the further you push toward the front, the richer the loot, the harsher the defenses. That geographical constraint concentrates the action, shrinks the distances, and turns every car into a closed arena. In principle, it's a clever answer to the reproach leveled at some extraction shooters of being walking simulators broken up by rare gunfights.

The other big difference is the scope of the crafting and the base. ARC Raiders keeps building on the periphery, as a reward between two raids. Enginefall makes it a central pillar. You place deployable bases on each level of the train, you erect walls and doors, you set up respawn points and storage, then you extract to your Dagger, that personal shuttle that serves as a rolling home and that you upgrade from rusty wreck to assault craft. Eventually you can even seize a fully drivable Marauder train, a chain of workshops, bunk rooms and gun decks. The ambition is colossal, and that's where the whole bet of the game plays out.

![Enginefall, the Dagger, home-vessel you upgrade between two extractions](/images/enginefall/screenshot-2.webp)

## What worries us

It's precisely on that pillar that the demo community is sounding the alarm, and it's worth hearing. The reproach comes back on a loop: the building system is a tracing of Rust, and it doesn't just accompany the extraction, it swallows it. Where the marketing hinted at light, secondary crafting, one player estimates the real split at fifty percent gathering and building for fifty percent raiding, when they expected thirty-seventy in favor of the action. For anyone coming for an extraction shooter, discovering a survival-crafter in disguise is a cold shower, and that kind of misunderstanding about the very nature of the game is the worst gift you can give a launch.

The rest of the feedback paints the classic picture of an ambitious project still green: connectivity issues, imperfect hit detection and collision boxes, experience bugs, and a resource scarcity that, while sometimes stemming from a player's lack of knowledge as the veterans retort, mostly betrays a faulty onboarding. Because the real alarm signal is there. Several testers dropped the game after two hours without understanding what it really offered, and were told to persevere. That's a bad answer. The first two hours are sacred, they must teach the systems and make you want more, and a game that demands faith to get past that mark condemns itself to keeping only its most hardened fans. The forced pacifism imposed on the solo player in crowded lobbies doesn't help.

Finally, that shift in vocabulary from playtest to demo isn't trivial. Showing a product in a botched state leaves a disastrous first impression there's no coming back from, and rebranding the exercise doesn't fix the copy. It's a quiet admission that the moment maybe wasn't right to expose itself.

![Enginefall, a richly defended first-class car, the summit of the progression](/images/enginefall/screenshot-3.webp)

## We're waiting, but with our eyes open

You have to separate the concept from its execution, and on the concept, Enginefall has everything going for it. The megatrain as a battlefield is one of the freshest ideas seen in the genre in a long time, and its social metaphor from the tail to first class gives it an identity few rivals can claim. Where ARC Raiders won by simply doing things right, as we wrote in [our dossier](/articles/arc-raiders-dossier), Enginefall attempts something riskier: marrying extraction to a real survival-crafter in an unheard-of setting. If the marriage holds, it'll be one of the most singular extraction shooters on the market.

But a great concept badly executed remains a bad game, and the demo shows too many cracks to hail genius just yet. Red Rover Interactive is holding a conceptual gem that needs a clear refocus on what it wants to be, a fully rethought onboarding and a big finishing job before it can claim to shake ARC Raiders. The potential is enormous, the ambition sincere, and the idea deserves its chance. We're waiting for the rest with curiosity and caution, hoping the studio listens to those who took the time to climb the train.
