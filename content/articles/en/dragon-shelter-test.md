The cozy farming game has become an aisle where you no longer stand out through mechanics but through what you put at the centre.

Dragon Shelter puts **cooking** there, and it is a smarter choice than it looks.

![Dragon Shelter, the farm and the dragons](/images/dragonshelter/screenshot-1.webp)

## The starting point

You take over your grandfather's abandoned farm, in a world where the bond between humans and dragons has been lost.

You grow crops, gather resources, craft goods, and transform neglected land into a sanctuary. And you raise dragons, each with unique traits and special powers, who help on the farm and beyond.

The studio stresses a detail I find lovely: treated well, with care and playtime, they become loyal companions who **sense people's needs** and lend their magic wherever it is missing.

## Cooking at the centre

Here is the structuring decision: cooking is the heart of crafting.

Meals strengthen bonds with neighbours, lift spirits and unlock new opportunities. Every harvest can be used in cooking, crafting, alchemy or trade, making each recipe meaningful.

And that solves a classic problem in the genre. In many farming games you grow to sell, and food is merely an intermediate currency. By making cooking the route to social relationships, the game gives you a reason to grow something other than whatever pays best.

## The town to lift

A nearby town has fallen into gloom, but hope remains.

With your dragons' help, you reconnect with the townsfolk, uncover their stories, and rekindle their sense of joy and community.

That is the genre's usual narrative structure, the village to awaken, and it works because it gives legible progression to an activity that could otherwise turn in circles.

Around it sits a fantasy world to explore, with magical biomes, curious creatures and hidden secrets.

![Dragon Shelter, cooking and recipes](/images/dragonshelter/screenshot-3.webp)

## What to know

The game launches today, developed by Wild Forest Studio and published by **Curve Games**, with no price displayed and no reviews as I write. It is single-player, with full controller support.

Curve's presence as publisher is an interesting indicator: it is a house that has often backed carefully made independent games rather than rushed productions, which creates a presumption of seriousness.

My reservation belongs to the genre, and it is severe because the competition is. A cozy farming game in 2026 has to exist beside titles with ten years of content and enormous communities. To hold on, you need either a better-tuned loop or a strong identity.

The dragons are the identity, and the idea of companions who perceive what residents need is charming. The question of duration remains: this genre is judged over forty hours, not three, and nobody can say anything about that on launch day.
