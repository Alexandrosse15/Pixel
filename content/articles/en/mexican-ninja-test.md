There are games you judge on restraint and games you judge on the total absence of it. Mexican Ninja belongs firmly to the second category, and it is better to know that before opening the page.

The pitch: Japan and Mexico collided in fire, power and betrayal, and Nuevo-Tokyo was born from it. A feudal regime was forged there between narcos and yakuzas, forming a new ruling class, the Narkuzas. They are the law, and the law, as the game puts it in its own words, thoroughly sucks.

![Mexican Ninja, the streets of Nuevo-Tokyo](/images/mexicanninja/screenshot-1.webp)

## What it is mechanically

A fast, chaotic 2.5D roguelike beat em up where every run unlocks new powers, builds and Mexican Jutsus.

The structure is the genre's: you cross the city, you take hits, you die, you start again with a bit more in your pockets. The game presents itself as pick-up-and-play while claiming a deceptive depth beneath the apparent simplicity.

What actually counts is what the studio puts forward as the foundation of combat: **precise movement, positioning and perfectly timed attacks decide every fight**.

That is the right promise for a beat em up. The difference between a button-masher and a good one sits exactly there: in the fact that placement matters as much as hitting, and that an attack's timing beats its quantity.

The streets, the studio promises, never give you a break, no matter how many have already fallen.

## The dressing, which is half the product

It has to be discussed because it is what sells the game.

The cultural blend is pushed to deliberate absurdity, and the game never apologises. The tone is that of a street storyteller pulling you aside to tell you something, with the Spanish turns of phrase left in the text as they are.

It is an owned B-movie aesthetic, and the question is not whether it is subtle but whether it holds. On the evidence of what the game shows, it does: the stylised streets of Nuevo-Tokyo have a real graphic personality, and the world is coherent in its excess.

Five Narkuza clan heads provide an objective structure, giving progression a spine many roguelikes of this type lack.

![Mexican Ninja, combat and the Jutsus](/images/mexicanninja/screenshot-2.webp)

## The reservations

The first risk with this kind of game is repetition. A run-based beat em up lives on the variety of its powers and on the feel of its strikes. If the Jutsus blur once the novelty passes, only the scenery remains.

The second is the tone itself. Outrageous humour works at full volume or not at all, and anyone who does not step into it in the first minute never will.

The third is more prosaic: the game launches today, developed by Madbricks and published by Amber Studio, without a single community review. Any assessment of longevity remains to be confirmed.

![Mexican Ninja, the five Narkuza heads](/images/mexicanninja/screenshot-3.webp)

## What sets the score

It rewards a world with immediate identity that it owns all the way through, a carefully styled street art direction, and above all a combat foundation putting positioning and tempo at the centre rather than hammering.

It accounts for the repetition risk inherent to the format, a divisive humour that will lose part of the audience, and a depth of content still to be verified beyond the opening hours.

If the concept makes you smile, it will probably deliver what it promises. If it makes you roll your eyes, walk on, the game will not try to convince you.
