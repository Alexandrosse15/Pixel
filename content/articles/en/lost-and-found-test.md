There is a line in LOST & FOUND's description that explains the mechanic better than any trailer.

"Sooner or later, you will realize that without proper organization of your workplace, everything you do is pointless."

That is exactly it. And it is why the game is smarter than it looks.

![LOST & FOUND, the lost property warehouse](/images/lostandfound/screenshot-1.webp)

## The job

Every day, lost items arrive at your warehouse. Your job is to help people get them back.

You pick up a form with a detailed description. You find the item. You return it.

The store page adds, with the appropriate punctuation: that's gonna be easy, right?

## Why this is not a hidden object game

Here is the trick.

In a classic hidden object game, the scene was built by somebody else. You scrutinise an image you did not compose, and your skill is purely visual.

Here, **you organised the warehouse.** You place the shelves, the bins, the containers. You decide what goes where.

Which means today's difficulty is not produced by the game, it is produced by you yesterday. A lazy layout on Monday is a lost afternoon on Thursday.

That is a rare loop, and it has a distant kinship with good logistics games: you are not playing against an opponent, you are playing against your past decisions. Except that here, instead of optimising a production chain, you are optimising an index.

I find that very well judged, and honestly a little cruel.

![LOST & FOUND, the shelves to organise](/images/lostandfound/screenshot-2.webp)

## The office and the cat

There is also an office to decorate. And here the studio does exactly the right thing: it announces the feature while mocking it.

You can set your favourite cat as wallpaper, or plaster the entire office with its photos. Does it serve any purpose? No, writes the studio, but it is fun.

A game that knows the difference between a mechanic and a pleasure, and is willing to offer both without pretending the second is the first, starts with my respect.

## Arcade mode, and multiplayer

This is the part I was not expecting.

There is an arcade mode, playable **in co-op or player versus player**, where you race to find the right item before time runs out.

The appeal is obvious the moment you think about it: hunting for an object in a warehouse is a silent, methodical solo activity, and it becomes complete disorder when four people are going through the same shelves shouting at each other.

In competitive, it poses an amusing question: do you look for your item, or do you watch what the other person is searching through?

The studio sums up its own pitch honestly: if you think working here is chaotic, wait until you see arcade mode.

![LOST & FOUND, arcade mode](/images/lostandfound/screenshot-3.webp)

## The standard caveat

The game came out about an hour before writing, with **nine reviews**, all positive, and around three hundred people playing.

Nine reviews are not a reception, and I will not pretend otherwise. Take this score for what it is: an assessment of the pitch and its coherence, to be confirmed once the game has lived a week.

The two things I will be watching.

**The depth of the descriptions.** All the game's tension rests on the form: a vague description makes searching frustrating, an over-precise one makes it mechanical. That is the hardest tuning there is and it does not show up immediately.

**And time pressure.** A tidying game that becomes a race against the clock loses what makes it restful. A tidying game with no pressure at all becomes a chore. The balance between the two will decide how long you stay.

![LOST & FOUND, the office to decorate](/images/lostandfound/screenshot-4.webp)

What I like, fundamentally, is the idea that your opponent is last week's you, the one who put a box in the wrong place because they were in a hurry.

We all have that colleague. In this game, it is you.
