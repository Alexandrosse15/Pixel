One clarification first, because it governs how to read this guide: the game launches today in early access. So there is no community value table, and no upgrade order proven across hundreds of hours.

This guide covers something else, the part that will not move: the logic of each system, the decisions that recur on every descent, and the mistakes the structure tends to provoke. The numbers will come once the community has mapped them.

![DIG, the workbench and blueprints](/images/dig/screenshot-2.webp)

## Understand the loop before you dig

All of DIG lives in a four-beat circuit worth holding in your head from the first minute.

You **descend**, you **collect**, you **climb out**, you **upgrade**. Then you go back down further.

What matters is understanding where the bottleneck is. It is not how fast you break rock, contrary to what instinct suggests. It is the pairing of **what you can carry** and **how long you can stay**.

A player who only upgrades the shovel digs quickly and comes back empty-handed or out of power. They optimised the part of the game that was not the problem.

## The three paths, and the order I recommend

The game offers three upgrades: the **shovel**, **storage capacity** and the **battery**. They are not equal, and the order completely changes the rhythm of the early hours.

### 1. Battery first

That is my main recommendation and it is counterintuitive.

The battery determines how long you can be down there. In a game where the good things are at the bottom, anything that lengthens your stay at the bottom mechanically increases the value of every descent.

Upgrading the battery does not make one descent better. It makes **every subsequent descent** better, including the ones where you hunt blueprints. It is an investment that compounds.

The sign that it is time: you regularly climb out because power ran low, not because the bag is full.

### 2. Storage next

The second bottleneck arrives fast. You stay down longer, so you collect more, so the bag fills before the battery empties.

The sign that it is time: you are leaving things on the ground, or climbing out with energy to spare.

That battery-storage alternation is the heart of progression. You swap between them by following whichever is holding you back, and you should never have both gauges emptying at the same moment. If that happens, good, you are balanced.

### 3. The shovel last, but not never

The shovel is the most satisfying upgrade and the least urgent. It reduces the time spent removing each layer, so it indirectly extends your endurance.

Its moment arrives when hard terrain appears. A weak shovel on rock turns your battery into wasted fuel: you burn time without gaining depth. That is the signal.

## Blueprints, and how to hunt them intelligently

Ancient blueprints unlock advanced technology and buried items. They are the only finds with a permanent effect on your run.

Three principles.

**They are deep.** The rule the studio states is simple: the deeper you go, the more the world opens up. A session spent raking widely near the surface yields resources, not blueprints. If you want blueprints, you have to descend, and therefore you need battery. It always comes back to the same point.

**They are worth more than loot.** Faced with a choice between hauling up a bag of common material or pushing a little further into unexplored ground, the logic favours exploration. Common material returns on every descent. An unlocked technology does not return, it is obtained.

**And they fund their own search.** Every blueprint that improves your efficiency makes hunting the next one easier. The first hours are therefore the slowest, and it accelerates markedly afterwards. If you find the opening sluggish, that is normal, do not judge the game on it.

## The decision that keeps coming back

On every descent you will hit this exact moment: the battery is low, the bag is not full, and there is clearly something one layer down.

Here is how I settle it.

**If the bag is nearly empty, push.** You have nothing to lose and everything to find. Climbing out with three rocks is a bigger failure than climbing out empty after seeing one more layer.

**If the bag is full of common material, climb out.** You have already won the descent. Greed here does not earn you a blueprint, it loses you what you are carrying.

**If you have a blueprint in the bag, climb out immediately.** That is the one absolute rule in this guide. One blueprint brought home beats two blueprints lost.

**And if you are torn, read the ground.** Dropping a layer in sand costs little. Dropping a layer in rock can consume the rest of your power. The question is not only whether there is something below, but what it will cost to reach.

## Matching your descent to the terrain

The game advertises distinct environments, notably rocky caves and sandy depths, and promises the adventure never feels the same twice.

In practice, two reflexes to build.

**On soft ground, go deep.** The cost per layer is low, so power should be spent descending rather than widening. This is where trips towards deep zones pay off.

**On hard ground, rake rather than drill.** When each layer is expensive, insisting on going down is the surest way to end up stranded far from the surface on a flat battery. Better to work the current layer horizontally.

The corollary is that you should read the ground before deciding your objective for the session. A descent without an objective is an average descent.

## Five mistakes that cost hours

**Putting everything into the shovel.** It is the most visible upgrade and the least structural early on. It has you digging quickly into an energy wall.

**Descending without knowing what you are after.** A resource session and a blueprint session do not play the same. The first works a known, profitable layer; the second charges into the unknown. Mixing them gives you the worst of both.

**Forgetting to craft.** The crafting system exists to convert your finds into efficiency. Hoarding materials without ever converting them is saving in a game that rewards investment.

**Always climbing out at the last possible second.** It looks optimal, it is actually the best way to lose an exceptional find that turned up thirty seconds too late. Keep a margin, especially on hard ground.

**And judging the game on the first hour.** The loop only becomes satisfying once two or three upgrades are in place. Before that, you are playing a throttled version of yourself.
