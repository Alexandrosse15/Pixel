Halfbrick Studios is Fruit Ninja. It is Jetpack Joyride. It is, for an entire generation, the studio of the games you launched at traffic lights.

Halfbrick Studios is publishing and co-developing Bruisers, a boxing game in which your character **ages in real time**, where injuries accumulate across a whole career, and whose store page warns that some consequences here are fatal.

I cannot think of a sharper turn in the industry this year.

![Bruisers, a fight in the ring](/images/bruisers/screenshot-1.webp)

The game enters early access on 21 October. No score today.

## Do not let the pixel art fool you

That is the warning the studio puts at the top of its own description, and it is the right summary.

The boxing is described as real: you slip, you counter, you move your head, you control distance, you break your opponent down.

That last verb matters. Breaking your opponent down rather than removing health points describes a boxing where the body degrades over the course of a fight, not one where two bars descend. If the system keeps that promise, we are in a rare family: fighting games that simulate wear.

## What happens outside the ring

And this is where it gets interesting, because the list is long and not decorative.

**Plan your meals. Make weight. Watch your vitals. Hold down a job between fights.**

Read that last one. A day job between fights. In a boxing game.

That is the line that tells you everything: Bruisers is not selling glory, it is selling the condition of being a boxer. The circuits build up gradually, from amateur to professional and then to bareknuckle, and you have to stock your corner, meaning your team of cutmen.

**Your boxer ages in real time.** The years wear on you. So do the injuries, and they accumulate. A career full of hard fights eventually shows.

That is the central mechanic, and the one that intrigues me most. In nearly every career game, time is a resource you spend. Here, time is an extra opponent, and it never loses.

![Bruisers, training and preparation](/images/bruisers/screenshot-2.webp)

## Every decision compounds

The studio insists that every decision compounds. A bad camp, a missed weight cut, the wrong fight at the wrong time: it all matters and it all adds up.

Rivalries develop along the way, and some of them get personal.

And outside the ring the world has its own rules: shady dealings, illegal gambling, a mob presence just below the surface. The studio writes that there are "options" if you know where to look.

You can guess what that means. A boxing game that puts doping and corruption in the same sentence as character ageing and mortality is a game that has chosen its register, and it is not the arcade register.

![Bruisers, the professional circuit](/images/bruisers/screenshot-3.webp)

## What will need checking

**Does the boxing hold up mechanically?** The whole proposition rests on two-dimensional combat where slipping, guarding and distance produce legible exchanges. If fights come down to a war of attrition where you hammer a button, everything else becomes dressing.

**Does the career management contain real decisions?** Meals, weight, work, cutmen: that can be a system of tight trade-offs, or a set of bars to fill before each fight. The difference is enormous and you cannot guess it from a store page.

**And how long is a career?** A game whose subject is ageing has to give you time to grow attached. Too short and the mechanic produces nothing. Too long and you will quit before the end.

![Bruisers, a bareknuckle fight](/images/bruisers/screenshot-4.webp)

There is something cheering about a studio known for thirty-second games putting its name to a project about a body degrading over twenty years.

We will come back to it on 21 October, and if the boxing matches the intention, it will be one of the autumn's surprises.
