There are games nobody has heard of that have more players than most releases everybody writes about. OpenFront is one of them.

The studio claims **over a million monthly active players** on its browser version. It arrives on Steam today.

![OpenFront, the shared world map](/images/openfront/screenshot-1.webp)

## The principle

A massively multiplayer real-time strategy game where **hundreds of real players fight simultaneously** on a single shared world map.

Every match starts the same way: you control a small patch of territory on a map shared with hundreds of others. After that, it is up to you.

You expand into unclaimed land, pressure weaker neighbours, accumulate gold and build your military. Before somebody else does the same to you.

And the game goes all the way: ports, cities, missile defences, naval warfare to project power, and **nuclear strikes** as a match reaches its endgame.

## Why it works

Two reasons, and they are linked.

**It is immediately comprehensible.** The studio claims it: genuine strategic depth layered onto controls anyone can learn in minutes. That is exactly what most strategy games lack, confusing complexity with depth.

**And the economy matters as much as the army.** The game rewards smart play over raw aggression: overextend and you collapse, underinvest and you get steamrolled. That is the basic tension of every great conquest game, reduced to its most legible form.

On top sits a layer I like a great deal: **diplomacy is built in**. Ally requests let you broker truces with neighbours, which turns a melee of hundreds into a network of fragile agreements. The real game becomes knowing when to betray.

![OpenFront, territorial expansion](/images/openfront/screenshot-3.webp)

## The maps, which change the strategy

The studio sums it up well: every map is a different puzzle.

Europe and its choke points reward diplomacy and timing. Asia and its open terrain favour rapid expansion. The global map brings ocean control into the equation.

That is smarter than a cosmetic variation: geography imposes a playstyle, so the same strategy does not work everywhere.

## What to know

The game arrives on Steam today in early access, with no price displayed as I write, and with in-app purchases.

The Steam arrival brings faster launches, better performance, achievements and exclusive cosmetics. The game also offers crossplay, which is essential for a title whose population lives on the web.

Cosmetics are **visible to every player in the match**: territory patterns that change the look of every tile you control, hundreds of real-world flags. That is the right model for this kind of game, since monetisation sits on display rather than on power.

My reservation covers what cannot be judged on day one: the health of a massively multiplayer game depends entirely on the quality of its matches. A match of three hundred players where everybody ganks whoever leads is a very different experience from one where alliances hold.

But with a million monthly players behind it, this game has already proved it can fill its maps. That is more than most strategy games released this year can say.
