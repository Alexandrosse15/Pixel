The moment Archipeo got me was when I understood that my problem was not production.

I had wood. I had ore. I had buildings running. And my home island was starving because my two freighters were spending their lives doing the same route in the wrong order.

Sound familiar? Welcome, we are going to get along.

![Archipeo, a first colonised island](/images/archipeo/screenshot-1.webp)

## What it is

A city builder and real-time strategy game with a retro look, wearing its references openly: Anno and The Settlers, named by the developer.

You start on a single island in a procedurally generated ocean world made of thousands of islands. Each has its own biome, resources and terrain, which means you cannot decide your strategy in advance. You explore, you discover, you adapt. And the store page's warning is plain: the world might not be empty.

Buildings start as simple foundations and grow into complex production chains. You optimise layouts, balance resource flows, unlock upgrades, specialise whole islands into a single job.

That is the part you were expecting and it is well made. It is not the interesting part.

## Haulage is the game

The layer that makes the difference is the fleet.

Your ships transport goods, defend your territory or project power far away. The developer makes a point of the large-scale pathfinding, and is right to, because the practical consequence is that you can automate freight between islands, run patrols, escort critical convoys.

In an island game, automating transport is not a convenience, it is the difficulty itself. On a continent, a badly drawn road costs seconds. Here, a badly assigned freighter costs an entire production chain, because nothing moves without a hull.

So I spent my first hours doing what I always do: building a mental spreadsheet. How many ships per route, which island makes what, where to put buffer storage. And I made exactly the classic mistake, specialising an island far too remote far too early.

![Archipeo, a fleet of freighters under way](/images/archipeo/screenshot-2.webp)

## Sixteen players, and that is the real surprise

Solo, co-op over LAN, or a multiplayer server for up to sixteen players.

You reveal other people's territory by exploring. You can define pacts between factions. You can share resources and claimed territory with allies, automate your defences, or start a large-scale war.

Sixteen players on a maritime logistics map is an idea that frightens and excites me in equal measure. Frightens, because that kind of session can become a synchronisation nightmare. Excites, because an island economy shared between allies, with convoys to escort and neighbours you do not yet know will be a market or a threat, is exactly the scenario the big names in the genre do not offer.

And here I have to be straight with you: the game launches today in early access. Sixteen-player infrastructure announced on day one by a developer whose store credit carries a single name needs proving over time. I could not run full lobbies under launch conditions, and nobody can until the population turns up.

![Archipeo, the archipelago's varied biomes](/images/archipeo/screenshot-3.webp)

## Now the reservations

The interface is dense and dry. When you are running fifteen islands you want overviews, filters, alerts that shout at you. There is the minimum and not yet the comfort.

Retro readability has a cost. The style is charming from a distance and becomes confused once an island is packed with buildings, which happens fast.

And naval combat is the weak link. Positioning fleets works, but the fighting itself lacks bite next to the care poured into the economy. You can tell where the developer's love went.

Finally, the procedural generation sometimes produces genuinely thankless starts: a tiny, poor island surrounded by empty neighbours. Restarting is free, but in a game that needs several hours to reveal its systems, you would like some guarantees about the opening map.

![Archipeo, a specialised island in full production](/images/archipeo/screenshot-4.webp)

One image stays with me: the moment my second port opened, the two freighters stopped passing each other empty, and my home island's food bar stopped falling. I built nothing beautiful that evening. I fixed a flow, and I got the satisfaction of an engineer who has found the leak.

If you are the sort who draws production chains on paper, come and confess it on [the InsertCoins Discord](https://discord.gg/473FE3dWvw), you will not be alone.
