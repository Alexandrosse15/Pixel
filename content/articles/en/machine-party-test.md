Some games strengthen friendships and others put them to the blood test. Machine Party proudly belongs to the second category. From the creator of Buckshot Roulette, this concentrate of minigames where failing can literally kill you turns a relaxed evening into a paranoid survival contest. For eight euros, it is one of the best ways to fall out with your loved ones in 2026.

![Machine Party, a lethal minigame between two to four players](/images/machineparty/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Machine Party is developed by Mike Klubnika, the brain behind the Buckshot Roulette phenomenon, supported by co-developer GDeavid, and published by Oro Interactive. It is available on PC via Steam at 7.99 euros. The premise: a collection of fifteen extremely violent minigames for two to four players online, where failure is not punished by a mere score but by a full-blown death. The creator's pedigree set real expectations, and good news, they are met.

## The gameplay

The heart of the game is that permanent tension between cooperation and sabotage. On paper, you face minigames together; in practice, each trial becomes a theatre of distrust where helping your neighbour and pushing them into the trap are two equally valid options. That ambiguity is Machine Party's great success: the game never forces you to betray, it just makes betrayal deliciously tempting, and it is from that temptation that the best moments are born, the ones where you scream with laughter watching a friend explode by your hand.

The Klubnika touch is immediately recognisable. As in Buckshot Roulette, the violence is not gratuitous, it is staged with a mechanical coldness that makes it both grating and hilarious. The fifteen minigames avoid repetition by varying registers, from pure reflex to booby-trapped coordination to trials that reward nerve as much as caution. The brevity of each round keeps the rhythm and the famous "one more game" pull going, all the more so as lethal death gives real weight to every mistake: here, you do not lose points, you lose a player.

![Machine Party, the tension between cooperation and sabotage among friends](/images/machineparty/screenshot-2.webp)

Where the game shows its limits is its intrinsically social nature. Machine Party is a memory machine among friends, but it is also a much emptier shell solo or with silent strangers. Without a complicit, chatty group, half the zest disappears, because it is the human reaction, the scream, the accusation, the fit of laughter, that turns a simple mechanic into a memorable moment. The game knows this and owns it, but you should be aware of it before buying: it is a game to be consumed in company, never alone.

The other reservation is content. Fifteen minigames is generous for the price, but the whole remains built for short, repeated sessions rather than dozens of hours in a row. Past a certain number of evenings, you will have gone through the surprises each trial holds, and replayability then rests entirely on group dynamics. That is the party-game lot, but it bounds the experience to what it is: an excellent evening trigger, not a game-universe.

## The tech

Technically, Machine Party owns a raw, mechanical art direction perfectly in tune with its subject. The cold, industrial aesthetic amplifies the joyous unease of the trials, and the staging of the violence, dry and unadorned, is exactly what lands. You recognise the visual signature of its creator, able to make a harmless machine deeply unsettling through atmosphere alone.

On the online co-op side, the whole works without major hitches, which is essential for a game that lives or dies on its multiplayer playability. The few technical reservations are minor and do not dent the pleasure: a bit more visual variety between some trials would have been welcome, but the coherence of the whole comes first, and the online stability is there to avoid ruining an evening.

![Machine Party, the cold, mechanical aesthetic signed by the Buckshot Roulette creator](/images/machineparty/screenshot-3.webp)

## The verdict

Machine Party confirms that Mike Klubnika has a rare talent for turning a simple, cruel idea into a memorable experience. Its fifteen lethal minigames, its permanent tension between mutual aid and betrayal, and its coldly hilarious staging make it one of the best evening triggers of the year, all for a trivial price. If you have a group of friends ready to kill each other in good spirits, go for it without hesitation.

Its limits are the owned ones of its genre: outside a complicit, chatty group, the magic fades, and its content is built for short sessions more than a solitary marathon. But in its register, that of the cruel party game to savour in company, Machine Party hits hard and true. It is mean, it is brilliant, and you will blame your friends as much as yourself. Exactly what we expected from the executioner of Buckshot Roulette.

**A party game as cruel as it is brilliant, provided you have friends to sacrifice on the altar of laughter.**

Score: 8/10
