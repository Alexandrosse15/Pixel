Feed The Forest's store page ends on a sentence I cannot shake: **blood is a type of juice**.

It arrives after a perfectly peaceful description in which a snail grows a forest in a desert. And that is the whole game: twenty minutes of ecological poetry, then a slippery slope into the most cynical optimising you have done in a while.

![Feed The Forest, the desert](/images/feedtheforest/screenshot-1.webp)

## The gentle part

You start with sand and a snail with powers. You plant. Plants produce. Production lets you plant elsewhere, and the desert retreats.

It is an active incremental, meaning you are not watching numbers rise over a coffee: you lay plants out on the map, and the arrangement matters. One species feeds its neighbour, another needs shade, and there are configurations markedly better than whatever you find on the first attempt.

The pleasure of painting a desert into a forest is real and slightly silly, in the good sense. One player puts it better than I can: it is atmospheric and relaxing, I love painting desert into forest.

## The part where it goes wrong

Then you automate. Then you hire minions. Then you unlock plants the game itself labels immoral, and you plant them anyway because they pay better.

That shift is the game's real idea. You have not changed mechanics, you have changed motivation without noticing, and the game points it out at the moment it is too late to claim you were greening the planet out of altruism.

It is light, it lasts a few hours, and it is considerably smarter than it looks.

![Feed The Forest, the plantations](/images/feedtheforest/screenshot-2.webp)

## What does not work

Two things, and they are linked.

**It is short.** Three to four hours for most players, seven for the most thorough. At 7.99 euros, several positive reviews still find the ratio a little tight, and I understand them.

**And it repeats.** One player, thumb up, writes that he felt he was doing the same thing for hours, that the visuals are beautiful and the plot somewhat chaotic. That is the limit of the incremental format: it lives on acceleration, and when the curve flattens, only the gesture is left.

That said, 92 per cent positive, and the ending comes up often in the praise. Somebody writes that they wished it were longer, but that the ending alone was worth it. Another, having finished the story, discovers with unfeigned joy that he unlocked no achievements and will have to start over.

![Feed The Forest, the forest](/images/feedtheforest/screenshot-3.webp)

## So, yes or no

Yes, if you want a game to finish across an evening and a half, if optimising rows of plants does not frighten you, and if you have room in your life for a snail.

No, if you expect a hundred-hour incremental to leave running in the background. That is not what this is, and it does not pretend otherwise.

5.99 euros during the launch discount. It is a small game that knows exactly what it is, stops before it tires you, and has the elegance to mock you one last time on the way out.

The best review on the page, incidentally, is three words repeated: there should be more games with snails. There should be more snails. Snails.

I am not going to pretend this review makes a stronger case.
