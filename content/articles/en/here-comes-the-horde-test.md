King Aubraque Hémar III has a very clear military strategy: stay behind the walls. His advisors put it tactfully in the launch post: "We understand that thou must hide... I mean, remain safe behind the walls to preserve the royal bloodline."

That is the whole spirit of Here Comes the Horde, from Polished Games (who also made Be My Horde). You do not fight. You spend gold, and peasants die in your name.

![The battlefield packed with units, with a burst of flame in the middle](/images/herecomesthehorde/screenshot-2.webp)

It is an incremental auto-battler. The necromancer Moriana sends her hordes at your castle, you build structures that produce soldiers (peasants, archers, knights, cavalry, mages), and the fight plays out on its own. Every undead killed earns gold. Between levels, harvested souls unlock new units and permanent upgrades.

And the show is the numbers. After a few levels, tens of thousands of units clash on screen, a grey tide against a colourful one. It is hypnotic. And once you unlock the special abilities, tornadoes and exploding sheep included, it is genuinely funny.

![The upgrade screen, on a blue stone wall](/images/herecomesthehorde/screenshot-4.webp)

The verbal sparring between the king and Moriana is fully voiced, and it gives real character to what could have been an animated spreadsheet. The tone is light, a little Monty Python.

The limit is the genre's: you watch far more than you play. Decisions happen between waves, and once you find the right upgrade order, the thirty levels roll by without much surprise. It is a game to run in the background, or for a quiet evening, not a strategy session.

![Knights and archers facing the horde, in the mud](/images/herecomesthehorde/screenshot-5.webp)

At 4.89 euros with the launch discount, it is a modest little pleasure. Nearly 1,500 players had jumped in a few hours after launch, and the Next Fest demo had already drawn more than 10,000 people.
