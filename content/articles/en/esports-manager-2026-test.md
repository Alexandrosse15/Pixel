There's a phrase that came up on a loop under the announcement of Esports Manager 2026, and it sums up everything: "I'm a little ashamed to say I'd play it." So are we. Because the idea of spending your evenings managing spreadsheets for a CS2 team instead of playing yourself has something absurd about it. Except we launched the game, and three hours later, we were still negotiating a contract extension at three in the morning. The shame gave way to addiction.

![Esports Manager 2026, the dashboard of an esports organization to manage down to the smallest detail](/images/esportsmanager2026/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Esports Manager 2026 is a management simulation developed by Neurona Games and published by indie.io, released on 6 July 2026 on PC. The principle is crystal clear for anyone who's ever touched Football Manager: you don't play the matches, you run the organization that wins them. Recruitment, contracts, training, staff, sponsors, tactics, everything goes through you. The game openly targets the Counter-Strike 2 scene, leans on real teams and real professional players, and even brings aboard well-known figures of the circuit like host James Banks, commentator Ne0kai or coach BIT to establish its credibility. A demo covering roughly 70% of the systems is already running, and it was enough to win over the skeptics.

## What it brings: a void finally filled

You have to measure where we're starting from. The last good Counter-Strike management game dates back some fifteen years, to the era of the old CS-Manager some still worship. Since then, the esports scene has exploded, it moves millions, fills arenas, and yet no one had delivered the Football Manager it deserved. It's that gaping hole Esports Manager 2026 comes to fill, and for that alone, it deserves our attention. This isn't yet another lazy clone, but a real attempt to take the genre seriously and apply it to a sport that didn't exist when Football Manager laid down its rules.

The great success is the depth of the systems. You scout players on a living market, you negotiate contracts and loans, you juggle a budget that sponsors feed according to performance and brand image. You hire specialized staff, analysts, coaches, media managers, each concretely influencing the squad's progress. And above all, you build weekly training plans that target not only mechanical skill but also player well-being, with psychology and cohesion sessions to avoid the burnout that decimates real teams. That human dimension, the idea that a player is a fragile athlete and not a statistic, is what lifts the game above the simple spreadsheet.

![Esports Manager 2026, the contract negotiation where every clause weighs on the budget and the squad](/images/esportsmanager2026/screenshot-2.webp)

## The gameplay: preparation before the thrill

As in any good management game, the bulk of the pleasure plays out upstream of the match. You spend your weeks preparing, adjusting, anticipating, and the match becomes the moment of truth where you find out whether your choices hold up. Esports Manager 2026 offers a simulation module where you control the tactics and react to the match's dynamic events, which gives a real sense of influence during the clashes rather than a result thrown in your face. Deciding on a ban, adjusting a round strategy, reacting to an opponent taking the lead: you find the tension proper to the genre, that exhilarating helplessness of the coach who can now only watch and hope.

That's where the game keeps its most important promise, the one about making you feel the weight of decisions. A refused extension, a youngster pushed into the starting lineup too soon, a badly calibrated training plan: it all gets paid for over the long term, and that causality is the engine that makes you relaunch one more season. The game has no scripted campaign, it plays out as long as your manager is standing, and that open structure is perfect for the genre. You project your own ambitions onto it, you write your own dynasty, and that's exactly what you ask of a simulation of this kind.

## What's missing to make it truly great

Still, the game drags a few weights that keep it out of the reference court, and the first is visual as much as symbolic. Esports Manager 2026 uses AI-generated player faces and logos, and it shows. In a game that bets everything on authenticity, on the thrill of managing real pros and real teams, that artificial crutch rings false and gives, at times, that cheap impression the fans of the old CS-Manager feared. When you sell the authentic, every synthetic face is a wrong note.

The rest owes to the youth of the project. The tactical layer of the simulation module, however appealing on paper, would gain from offering more real levers to influence a match, on pain of turning into an illusion of control once you've seen its bottom. On the technical side, the first players flagged telling details, like a demo that ran with no frame limiter and heated machines to a thousand FPS on menus, the kind of negligence that betrays a studio still finding its feet. Nothing dramatic, but the accumulation reminds you you're holding a promising base more than a finished monument. We'd also love to see the game answer the community's requests, like managing several teams in parallel, which would considerably flesh out the replayability.

![Esports Manager 2026, the simulation module where you adjust the tactics and react to the match's flow](/images/esportsmanager2026/screenshot-3.webp)

## What we take away

Esports Manager 2026 is a good surprise and a real guilty pleasure. It takes a fantasy the CS2 scene has been quietly nursing for years, that of playing the coach who builds a championship team, and it finally gives it a frame worthy of the name. The depth of the management systems, the attention paid to player well-being and that addictive loop of preparation then truth on the server make it the best esports management game in a very, very long time. For a Counter-Strike fan who also has a slippered-strategist soul, it's a no-brainer.

But you have to be lucid about its status. It's an excellent starting point, not yet an achievement. The AI-generated faces dent the authenticity that is nonetheless its selling point, the tactical layer needs deepening, and a few technical bits of negligence remind you you're facing a studio that's learning. It's an honest, endearing game, cut for genre and scene diehards, but one that will have to grow, season after season, to become the Football Manager of esports everyone is really waiting for. As it stands, we're already playing it with a guilty smile, and that's maybe the finest compliment we can pay it.

## Verdict

The CS2 management game we've waited fifteen years for, deep and addictive despite its digital cardboard faces: a guilty pleasure for the fans, a solid base begging to mature.

**Strengths:**

- Real management depth worthy of a Football Manager
- The human dimension, player well-being and psychology at the heart of the game
- A simulation module where you weigh tactically on the matches
- Finally fills a fifteen-year void for the Counter-Strike scene

**Weaknesses:**

- AI-generated faces and logos that betray the authenticity argument
- An appealing tactical layer that still needs deepening
- A few bits of technical negligence that betray a studio finding its feet
- No multi-team management, a replayability we'd love to be broader
