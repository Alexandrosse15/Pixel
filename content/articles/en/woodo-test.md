There is a category of games whose purpose is not to occupy you but to slow you down. Woodo belongs to it, and it gets there through one very simple action: putting a wooden piece in the right place.

![Woodo, a diorama being assembled](/images/woodo/screenshot-1.webp)

## The action

You assemble wooden scenes piece by piece. As objects find their places, the scene **fills with colour** and comes to life, with animations and small surprises.

The studio stresses the sensation, and it is right to: every piece clicks into place satisfyingly, as though you were handling a real toy.

And assembly is not linear. You have to **turn and explore the three-dimensional space** to find hidden pieces, which turns a flat puzzle into an object you handle.

That is what separates Woodo from the thousand relaxing puzzle games released this year: here you are not ticking boxes, you are building something and watching it animate.

## The through-line

You follow Foxy and Ben the frog across a series of interconnected dioramas, each capturing a moment from their shared adventure.

A narrator's voice accompanies it, and the studio states its intention plainly: to recover a world where time was plentiful, where much of it was spent outdoors, where the simplest things surprised and every day promised great adventure.

That is a pitch that could be unbearably twee. It holds because the game does not over-explain and lets the assembly carry the feeling.

A clever detail: **the narration adapts to your pace**, and the game offers help when you need it. You will never be stuck, and you will never be rushed.

![Woodo, colouring in the scene](/images/woodo/screenshot-3.webp)

## What to know

The game launches today, developed by Tiny Monks Tales and published by Daedalic Entertainment, with no price displayed as I write and no reviews.

The comfort options are generous: adjustable text size, colour alternatives, fully playable with a mouse, no timed input, free saving.

My reservation belongs to the format. A diorama game ends, and it usually ends quickly. That is not a flaw if what you want is precisely a few calm evenings, but do not expect it to fill your autumn.

What stays with me is that it does one thing many cozy games get wrong: it gives slowness a reason. You do not go slowly because the game is thin, you go slowly because turning the object to find the hidden piece is the pleasure itself.
