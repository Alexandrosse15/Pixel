The mob promises you'll sleep with the fishes when you've talked to the wrong person. Don't Sleep with the Fishes takes the expression literally, drops you into a lifeboat in the middle of nowhere, and politely explains that sleep is now a high-risk activity. We spent the mandatory hour surviving, and came out soaked, a little shaken, and not entirely sure what had been watching us from the water.

![Don't Sleep with the Fishes, the opening wreck where every second on deck costs dearly](/images/dontsleepwiththefishes/screenshot-1.webp)

## The context

Don't Sleep with the Fishes is a survival horror point-and-click made by Taiki, published by DopplerGhost and released on this 26 June 2026 on PC. It costs two euros, lasts about an hour, and never once pretends otherwise. It's exactly the kind of micro-game you want to defend on principle: no season pass, no roadmap, no promise of a hundred hours of content. Just an idea, an atmosphere, and the decency to stop before it overstays its welcome.

## Salvage what you can, abandon the rest

It all starts with a shipwreck, and that's the game's best idea. Something strikes your vessel on a discreet delivery run, the hull gives way, and the countdown starts with no warning. You have a few seconds to ransack the sinking deck and toss into your lifeboat whatever your hand grabs. A can of food, a lamp, a knife, a flare: every item you grab is an item that will weigh in later, and every second spent hesitating is water rising. We replayed that opening several times just to grab a different inventory, and that's where the game shows its first good card. What you take aboard in thirty seconds of panic decides how you'll die three days later.

Then comes pure survival, cut into days. During the day, you spend an energy gauge fishing, eating, tinkering with the boat or chatting with your fellow castaway. When your energy runs dry, you decide to sleep, and that's where the title keeps its mob promise. At night, something can come. And when it does, all you have to get out of it is what you salvaged. No combat, no health bar: just an item to pull out at the right moment, or a final, terrible awakening.

![Don't Sleep with the Fishes, managing a full day between fishing, energy and choices that carry weight](/images/dontsleepwiththefishes/screenshot-2.webp)

## The loop that works, and the one that runs dry

The tension in Don't Sleep with the Fishes doesn't come from its graphics or a nicely animated monster. It comes from a very simple question it asks you every evening: do I sleep now, fragile but rested, or do I scrape one more action at the risk of collapsing with nothing in hand for the night. It's resource management dressed up as horror, and the disguise holds because the game refuses to reassure you. You never really know what's prowling, and that ignorance does all the work the budget couldn't pay for.

The trouble is the machinery lays itself bare very fast. In two or three runs, you've identified the items that save a night, you've understood which shipmate tells which story, and the randomness of the events starts going in circles. Horror runs on the unknown, and the unknown has a short shelf life when the content is this compact. What chilled you on the first run becomes a checklist by the fourth. That's the price of an hour-long game built around surprise: the second visit already pays less than the first, and the fifth feels like an audit.

We should also talk about the endings, because that's the official replayability argument. There are several, three shipmates to bring along, and most outcomes see you end up at the bottom. On paper, that's exactly what you want from a branching game. In practice, several endings resemble each other enough that you feel you're relaunching mostly to tick a box, not to discover a real variation. The promise multiplies the doors; behind them, some rooms are furnished identically.

## Corner-of-the-page horror

The writing is the other real success, and it plays exactly in the register we hoped for: offbeat without being ridiculous. Your exchanges with the lifeboat companion swing between the grim pragmatism of two castaways rationing a can of tuna and a dull unease that builds night after night. The game doesn't explain what sank the ship, nor what hides beneath the surface, and it has the wisdom never to lift the veil entirely. Horror here isn't spectacular, it's domestic: it's the dread of closing your eyes when you know something has noticed you're there.

It's a tone that's hard to hold, and Don't Sleep with the Fishes holds it better than productions ten times more expensive. It understands that the most effective fear isn't the monster shown, but the decision to sleep anyway. Still, it all crumbles a bit once you've seen the strings. Once the mystery is spent, the dialogue that made you shiver becomes lines you skip to get to the next morning faster.

## The tech: tiny and owned

Let's be clear about what you're buying. Don't Sleep with the Fishes is a modest point-and-click, no voice acting, English only, with a handful of screens. Visually, it bets everything on atmosphere rather than resolution, and it's the right call: the gloom, the lapping water and the off-screen do more for the fear than an animation budget that doesn't exist. The jump scares are there, dosed with a restraint that's surprising for a title of this scale, and they fall just rarely enough to keep their effect.

For comfort, we hit no crash or blocking bug during our sessions, which is already a win for a two-euro game. The lack of a French localization will close the door on part of the audience, and that's a shame for a title that leans so hard on its text. It's the owned limit of a solo production that chose to polish its atmosphere rather than flaunt its means, and within that contract, it does its part.

## What we take away

Don't Sleep with the Fishes does one thing and does it well: it turns the most banal act in the world, falling asleep, into an anxious gamble, and it sells it for the price of a coffee. The opening wreck is a great piece of game design, the survive-or-decide loop holds for its natural length, and the offbeat writing installs an unease that many far better-funded horror games never reach. For sixty minutes, it's an honest, genuinely likeable proposition.

But we won't pretend: the depth runs dry faster than you'd want. The pool of items still lacks variety to sustain the dozens of hours the genre promises, a few builds dominate too clearly, and the concept naturally locks out those who aren't comfortable. It's an excellent game of one to ten hours that struggles to justify the next one. For two euros, that's exactly the deal you sign, and you sign it without regret.

## Verdict

An hour of anxious shipwreck for the price of a coffee: tiny, clever, quickly seen through, but you sleep worse afterwards, and that's all it promised.

**Strengths:**

- The opening wreck, where every item grabbed in panic decides your survival
- The sleep-or-not loop that turns energy management into dread
- Offbeat writing that installs real unease while showing nothing
- An unbeatable length-to-price ratio on the first run

**Weaknesses:**

- The mystery runs dry the moment you see the strings, which is very fast
- Multiple endings, several of which resemble each other too closely
- A concept that inherently excludes players uncomfortable at the helm
