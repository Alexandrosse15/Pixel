One detail convinced me Demockracy knew what it was talking about: the parliament has 55 seats, allocated proportionally using the D'Hondt method. Not an abstract popularity bar, a real electoral rule, the one that favours big parties and forces everyone else to negotiate. 28 seats and you govern alone. 27, and you spend election night begging for ministries.

You lead a party in a fictional country split into four regions and twenty districts. The goal is not to govern well. The goal is to win, then hold on to power.

![The country map and its districts, with polls by party](/images/demockracy/screenshot-1.webp)

## Clean hands or trolls

Every objective has two paths. A rally, or an army of trolls. Persuade a rival, or blackmail them. Dirty methods are faster and fill a risk gauge, and when it overflows, scandals hit: empty coffers, resigning MPs, vanishing voters. It is simple, readable, and creates exactly the temptation it should.

The most interesting part comes after the election. Coalitions are genuinely negotiated, with up to two partners demanding ministries and setting red lines. Each partner has a harmony score: keeping a red line earns 12 points, breaking one costs 25, passing a law they told you not to pass costs 40. Below 35, an ultimatum. I love dry numbers like these, which turn politics into game mechanics without making a cartoon of it.

![An event: protesters at the palace gates, and the response options](/images/demockracy/screenshot-3.webp)

The solo developer added a presidency with a veto and a two-round election two days before launch. That is impressive, and slightly worrying: this is a game still growing very fast, with systems that have not all had time to settle. The interface, all terminal green on black, is austere and crowded.

And it has to be said: the Steam page discloses that the portraits, event illustrations and region artwork are generated images, as is part of the localisation. It shows in the politicians' faces, all cut from the same mould.

![The party doctrine tree](/images/demockracy/screenshot-4.webp)

At 4.87 euros, it is still one of the subtlest political games I have seen this year in substance. If you like Suzerain or Democracy and can tolerate a poor presentation, there is a real engine here.
