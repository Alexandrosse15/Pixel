Let us start with the mystery, because it is real and the answer explains a good deal about this game.

GRIT has existed since 2021. People were talking about it, it had a page, screenshots, a community. And it launches on Steam today, 18 August 2026, with **zero reviews** and a single post on its news feed, published the day before yesterday. A page that looks brand new for a five-year-old game is odd. Here is why.

![GRIT, the Wild West and hundred-player firefights](/images/grit/screenshot-1.webp)

## The explanation: it was not allowed to be there

In June 2022, at the Galaverse convention, Epic Games and **Gala Games** announced their collaboration on a game: GRIT. It became the **first blockchain game on the Epic Games Store**, with player-owned assets as NFTs, bought and resold, and a drop of ten thousand avatars to be minted through Gala Games. The game was announced as free to play.

Meanwhile, Steam was closed to it. In **October 2021**, Valve had settled the matter by outright banning from its store any game built on blockchain, cryptocurrency or NFTs. GRIT ticked every box of that ban.

That is why a 2021 game arrives on Steam in 2026: it could not come earlier.

## What had to be dropped to get in

This is where it gets interesting, and it can be verified in thirty seconds.

I combed the current Steam description for the words NFT, blockchain, crypto, token, wallet, Gala, mint and web3. **Not one occurrence. Not a single one.**

Valve's rule has not changed since 2021. So to be here today, GRIT has had to part with what was its main selling point four years ago.

The business model followed the same path. Gone is the free game funded by resellable assets: GRIT is now **a paid game**, monetised through a thoroughly conventional **CattlePass**, a hundred levels of weapon skins, horse skins, parachutes, outfits and titles, the first one included with the game, plus eight themed Logbooks and a daily mission.

In other words, we have a game that traded the promise of digital ownership for a battle pass like fifty others. You can find that disappointing. I mostly think it was the only choice that gave it a shot at reaching a real audience, and that the page's total silence on the subject is telling.

Worth recalling for the record that the press was not kind at the time: Kotaku ran a headline stating that the Epic Store's first blockchain game looked dreadful. The game has had four years to prove them wrong.

![GRIT, the four suits and the weapon system](/images/grit/screenshot-2.webp)

## The idea that sells the dream, and it works

On to the game itself, because its central idea is excellent and deserves better than its past.

**Every gun you pick up is also a playing card.** You have four slots. Each gun carries a suit. Two of a suit switches a perk on, four makes you something the rest of the lobby has to plan around.

That turns every looted body into a concrete dilemma: take the better gun, or the one that completes the hand? It is simple, immediately legible, and it gives loot a meaning most battle royales have never managed to give it.

The four suits map to four roles:

**Diamonds, the Tracker.** Nobody gets to hide. Hit someone and he stays lit up for your whole posse. Full hand, and anyone shooting at you gives himself away doing it.

**Hearts, the Chaplain.** Everything that keeps people alive happens faster around you. Full hand, your remedies carry to whoever is close and the men you revive come back stronger.

**Spades, the Merchant.** Armour becomes a suggestion, your lead goes through what other men were counting on, and a full hand cuts the whole posse in.

**Clubs, the Cavalry.** The horse is not how you get to the fight, it is how you win it. Faster, tougher, eventually near unkillable, with the ability to haul teammates up at a gallop.

The detail that makes the system smart: **suit perks spill onto teammates**. A posse that coordinates its hands beats one that loots at random. Here is a battle royale that rewards talking over twitch.

## The rest of the formula

A hundred players a match, around twenty-five minutes a round, solo, duos or posses.

The closing zone is called the **Calamity**, and the game adds an idea I have not seen elsewhere: **a train that keeps rolling straight through it**. You can board it for loot, stay aboard to hold position inside the ring, and throw off anyone who had the same thought. While everyone else runs, you ride.

Better still, the variation between matches is structured rather than left to loot luck: **three Wildcards are dealt from a deck of twenty before a shot is fired**. The Calamity might chase the train instead of closing a circle, Second Chances might be off the table, the Deathcart might be stocked with something it has no business selling. You find out the day's rules as the cards hit the table.

And being gunned down does not end your match: you duel your way back.

![GRIT, the Calamity and the train crossing the map](/images/grit/screenshot-3.webp)

## What sets the score

It rewards a genuinely good design idea, the inventory as a poker hand, which gives every pickup meaning and pushes teams to coordinate. The Wildcards dealt before the match and the train inside the zone are two more finds, and the western setting remains badly underused in the genre.

It marks down a context that does not inspire confidence. A paid hundred-player battle royale lives or dies on population, and this one arrives with a four-year history, a complete change of business model, and an original audience that came for resellable assets which no longer exist. Empty servers are the first risk of all.

It also accounts for the page's silence about that past. Not mentioning NFTs is entirely legal and probably mandatory to be on Steam, but a 2022 buyer deserves to know what is left of what was promised.

So the advice is cautious: the idea is worth trying, and it is worth checking the concurrent player count before paying.
