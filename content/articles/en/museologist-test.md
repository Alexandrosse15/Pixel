An earthquake hit last night. The display cases are in shards, the marble plinths are cracked in half, and somewhere under the rubble there is a swordsmith's blade, a pharaoh's death mask and the skull of a creature that has not walked the Earth in sixty million years.

You have seven days. At the end of those seven days, the board decides whether the museum reopens or closes for good.

That is a very good setup. It is the best thing in the game.

![Museologist, a wrecked exhibit hall](/images/museologist/screenshot-1.webp)

## What you actually do

You pick up a bit of something off the floor. You look at it. The game draws a glowing outline where it goes. You put it there.

Repeat 3,105 times.

That number is not me being unkind, it is written on the store page: 3,105 fragments making up 428 objects. The developers are proud of it, and I understand why, because that is a colossal amount of cutting up. But advertising a volume of chores as a selling point also tells you very plainly what you are coming for.

The good news is that the feeling lands. There is a dumb, genuine pleasure in watching a hall go from rubble field to something you would pay to visit. The broken glass disappears, the pedestals straighten, and the room's ambient sound shifts as it fills up. That audio detail is the best idea in the game, the one you do not notice in the moment and that keeps you going.

## The board, or the only real tension

Every finished hall is scored on accuracy, not speed. A sloppy placement drags your rating down, and the rating decides whether the museum reopens.

On paper that is the mechanic that lifts the game above a checklist. In practice it deflates fast, because the glowing outline tells you where each piece goes. It is hard to get something wrong when the answer is highlighted. I spent my first hours wondering where the punishment was, and I never once felt the board behind me.

![Museologist, a restored hall](/images/museologist/screenshot-2.webp)

## The gadgets that play for you

And here is a problem I find genuinely fascinating.

As you clear halls, you unlock kit: delivery drones, an AR visor, an auto-snap tool.

Read carefully what those three things do.

The drones: you hold a fragment, you release them, they go and find every other piece of the same object and bring them to your hands. The visor: it highlights the pieces belonging to the same object so you do not have to look for them. Auto-snap: it locks the fragment instantly into its correct position, no manual alignment.

In other words, progression consists of unlocking tools that successively remove the only three things you were doing: searching, identifying, placing.

I am not saying it is badly done. By hall twelve it is a relief. I am saying it is the most honest admission a tidying game has made in a long time: it worked out that its loop gets tiring, and it built the tiredness into its upgrade tree.

## The order of the halls

The only place where you make a decision is choosing the next hall and stacking your abilities. Which wing first, which gadgets together. That gives the run a personal rhythm, and it is more than an illusion of choice, but it is not really a system either.

![Museologist, fragments waiting to be assembled](/images/museologist/screenshot-3.webp)

My best moment, and I will hand it over because it is ridiculous: the dinosaur skull. You assemble a three-metre thing, bone by bone, with no manual, and when the last vertebra clicks in you step back to look at it. For twenty seconds I was a palaeontologist. No tidying game had ever done that to me.

The rest of the time I was an employee who had been issued a visor that finds things.

![Museologist, the central hall of the museum](/images/museologist/screenshot-4.webp)

If what you want is the thing that runs in the background of a podcast and rewards you every four minutes, this is exactly the product. If you want restoring a museum to demand knowledge, hesitation and the risk of being wrong, look elsewhere, the glowing outline is waiting for you.
